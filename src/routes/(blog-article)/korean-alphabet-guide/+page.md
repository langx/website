The Korean alphabet, Hangul, has 24 basic letters: 14 consonants and 10 vowels. It was designed on purpose in the 15th century under King Sejong, and the letters are packed into square blocks, one block per syllable. Learn the 24 letters, then the doubled consonants and combined vowels, then how blocks are built, and you can sound out real Korean words, often within a few days of steady practice.

This guide uses the letters, names and sounds from our free [Korean alphabet chart](/tools/alphabet/korean), and every practice word comes from our list of the [5,927 most common Korean words](/tools/most-common-words/korean), with its rank on that list.

## What kind of script is Hangul?

Most alphabets grew slowly out of older scripts. Hangul did not. Our alphabet data calls it **featural**: the shape of a letter shows how you make the sound.

- **Consonants picture the mouth.** ㄱ shows the back of the tongue raised to block the throat, ㄴ the tongue tip touching the ridge behind the teeth, ㅁ the closed mouth, ㅅ a tooth and ㅇ the open throat. Other consonants add strokes to these five: ㄴ becomes ㄷ and then ㅌ, ㅁ becomes ㅂ and then ㅍ, ㅅ becomes ㅈ and then ㅊ.
- **Vowels are built from three ideas.** A dot for heaven (today written as a short stroke), a flat line for earth (ㅡ) and a standing line for a person (ㅣ). Put the short stroke on the right of ㅣ and you get ㅏ (a); on the left, ㅓ (eo).

Korean is still an alphabet in the strict sense: vowels and consonants are separate, equal letters. What makes it look unlike English is the layout. Instead of writing letters in a row, you pack each syllable into a square, so 한 (han) is three letters, ㅎ + ㅏ + ㄴ, in one block.

## A short history: King Sejong and the 15th century

Before Hangul, Korean was written with Chinese characters, which took years to learn and did not fit Korean grammar well. King Sejong the Great of the Joseon dynasty set out to give ordinary people a script they could learn quickly. The royal records say that in the last month of 1443 the king devised 28 letters, and the script was published in 1446 in a document called *Hunminjeongeum*, "the correct sounds for the instruction of the people."

Four of those 28 letters later fell out of use, which leaves the 24 basic letters taught today. South Korea celebrates the script every year on Hangul Day, October 9.

## The 14 basic Korean consonants

These are the consonants on our [Hangul chart](/tools/alphabet/korean). Sounds are rough English approximations, enough to sound a word out.

| Letter | Name | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ㄱ | giyeok | g or k |
| ㄴ | nieun | n |
| ㄷ | digeut | d or t |
| ㄹ | rieul | between r and l |
| ㅁ | mieum | m |
| ㅂ | bieup | b or p |
| ㅅ | siot | s |
| ㅇ | ieung | silent at the start, ng at the end |
| ㅈ | jieut | j |
| ㅊ | chieut | ch |
| ㅋ | kieuk | k with a puff of air |
| ㅌ | tieut | t with a puff of air |
| ㅍ | pieup | p with a puff of air |
| ㅎ | hieut | h |

A few of them need a note:

- **ㄱ, ㄷ, ㅂ and ㅈ** sit between two English sounds. At the start of a word they sound closer to a soft k, t, p and ch; between two vowels they sound like g, d, b and j. Koreans hear them as one sound, so don't worry about it at first.
- **ㄹ** is a quick flap, close to the tt in American "butter", at the start of a syllable, and an l at the end of one.
- **ㅇ** does two jobs. At the start of a block it is silent and only fills the space, because a block cannot begin with a vowel. At the end of a block it is *ng*, as in "sing".

## The 10 basic Korean vowels

| Letter | Name | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ㅏ | a | a in father |
| ㅑ | ya | ya in yard |
| ㅓ | eo | u in but |
| ㅕ | yeo | yu in yummy |
| ㅗ | o | o in more |
| ㅛ | yo | yo in yodel |
| ㅜ | u | oo in boot |
| ㅠ | yu | you |
| ㅡ | eu | oo said with a flat mouth |
| ㅣ | i | ee in see |

Look for the pattern: one short stroke is a plain vowel, two short strokes add a *y* in front. ㅏ is a and ㅑ is ya, ㅗ is o and ㅛ is yo. That turns ten shapes into five pairs.

Notice also which way each vowel points. ㅏ ㅑ ㅓ ㅕ ㅣ are **vertical**, and ㅗ ㅛ ㅜ ㅠ ㅡ are **horizontal**. That decides where the vowel goes in a syllable block, as you'll see below.

### Combined vowels

The chart lists the 10 basic vowels. Real Korean also joins them into 11 combined vowels, which is how the full set reaches 21 vowels:

| Vowel | Built from | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ㅐ | ㅏ + ㅣ | e in bet |
| ㅔ | ㅓ + ㅣ | e in bet |
| ㅒ, ㅖ | ㅑ or ㅕ + ㅣ | ye in yes |
| ㅘ | ㅗ + ㅏ | wa in wander |
| ㅙ, ㅚ, ㅞ | ㅗ or ㅜ + another vowel | we in wet |
| ㅝ | ㅜ + ㅓ | wo in won |
| ㅟ | ㅜ + ㅣ | wee |
| ㅢ | ㅡ + ㅣ | eu gliding into ee |

In everyday Seoul speech ㅐ and ㅔ sound almost the same, and so do ㅙ, ㅚ and ㅞ. You still have to spell them correctly, so learn each word's spelling as you meet it.

## Tense and aspirated consonants

This is the part of Hangul that English speakers find hardest, because English has no separate letters for these sounds.

Korean has three versions of several consonants:

- **Plain**: ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ ㅈ, said softly with a little air.
- **Aspirated**: ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅊ, said with a strong puff of air, like the k in "kit" if you hold a hand in front of your mouth.
- **Tense**: ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅉ, and ㅆ for s. These are written by doubling the plain letter. Say them with a tight throat and no puff of air, a little like the k in "sky" or the p in "spy".

The five doubled letters, added to the 14 basic ones, give the 19 consonants of the full set.

| Plain | Aspirated | Tense |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ㄱ | ㅋ | ㄲ |
| ㄷ | ㅌ | ㄸ |
| ㅂ | ㅍ | ㅃ |
| ㅈ | ㅊ | ㅉ |
| ㅅ | none | ㅆ |

The difference changes the meaning. Two sets of words from our list show it:

| Plain | Aspirated | Tense |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 달 (moon, #432) | 탈 (mask, #822) | 딸 (daughter, #408) |
| 불 (fire, #548) | 풀 (grass, #1142) | 뿔 (horn, #4260) |

<Callout type="info">You won't learn to hear 달, 탈 and 딸 apart from a table. Say each one into a voice message and ask a native speaker which word they heard. It is the fastest way to find out whether your tense consonants are really tense.</Callout>

## How letters stack into syllable blocks

Every Korean syllable is one square block, and every block starts with a consonant. There are only a few layouts:

1. **Consonant + vertical vowel, side by side.** ㄴ + ㅏ = 나 (na). ㅎ + ㅣ = 히 (hi).
2. **Consonant + horizontal vowel, stacked.** ㄴ + ㅜ = 누 (nu). ㄱ + ㅗ = 고 (go).
3. **Either of those plus a final consonant underneath.** ㅎ + ㅏ + ㄴ = 한 (han). ㄱ + ㅡ + ㄹ = 글 (geul).

Put the last two together and you have 한글, the name of the alphabet itself (#1554 on our list).

When a syllable starts with a vowel sound, the silent ㅇ fills the consonant slot. That's why 아 is simply "a" and 우 is simply "u". Combined vowels wrap around: ㅇ + ㅘ = 와 (wa), with ㅗ under the ㅇ and ㅏ on the right.

You read each block top-left first, then right, then down, and the blocks themselves run left to right like English.

## Batchim: final consonants, briefly

The consonant at the bottom of a block is called **batchim** (받침). Almost any consonant can sit there, but at the end of a syllable they collapse into just seven sounds:

| Final sound | Written as |
| --- | --- |
| k | ㄱ, ㅋ, ㄲ |
| n | ㄴ |
| t | ㄷ, ㅌ, ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅎ |
| l | ㄹ |
| m | ㅁ |
| p | ㅂ, ㅍ |
| ng | ㅇ |

The final k, t and p are not released: your mouth closes on the sound and stops. So 꽃 (flower, #976) is said *kkot*, with the ㅊ at the bottom sounding like a clipped t, and 밥 (cooked rice, #382) is *bap*, with the lips closing on the last p.

Two more things you'll notice soon. Some blocks have two consonants at the bottom, and usually only one of them is said. And when a block ending in a consonant is followed by one that starts with the silent ㅇ, the consonant slides over: 한국어 (the Korean language, #5789) is spelled han-guk-eo but said *han-gu-geo*. These are rules to pick up while reading, not to memorize before you start.

## FAQ

### How many letters are in the Korean alphabet?

Hangul has 24 basic letters: 14 consonants and 10 vowels. Counting the 5 doubled consonants and the 11 combined vowels as well gives 40 letters: 19 consonants and 21 vowels.

### Who invented the Korean alphabet?

It was created under King Sejong the Great of the Joseon dynasty. The script was devised in 1443 and published in 1446 in the *Hunminjeongeum*.

### How long does it take to learn Hangul?

Many learners can read Hangul slowly after a few days of focused study, because the letters are few and follow clear patterns. Reading fluently, and knowing what you're reading, takes much longer.

### What is batchim in Korean?

Batchim is the consonant written at the bottom of a syllable block, such as ㄴ in 한. At the end of a syllable it is said as one of seven sounds: k, n, t, l, m, p or ng.

### Why does ㅇ appear at the start of so many blocks?

Every block must start with a consonant. When a syllable begins with a vowel sound, the silent ㅇ fills that slot, so 아 is read "a". At the bottom of a block, ㅇ is pronounced ng.
