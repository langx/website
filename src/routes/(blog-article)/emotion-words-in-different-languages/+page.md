## Where these words come from

Each [/tools/say](/tools/say) page lists an English word in up to 53 languages. The words are ranked by how often they appear in film and TV subtitles (the OpenSubtitles 2018 frequency lists) and matched to English through Wiktionary. So these are words people actually say out loud, and every page shows where the word ranks in its language.

Two things to know before you read the tables:

- **We only print rows whose meaning matches.** Film dialogue is messy, and some pages match a word in a different sense. When the happy page matched Chinese 舒服 ("comfortable") or the angry page matched a word more often used for "war", we left that row out.
- **The lists are lowercase.** We capitalize German nouns like *Angst* the way you'd see them in a sentence.

## Why emotion words don't map one-to-one

A table makes it look as if every language has one neat word for each feeling. The data itself shows that it doesn't.

- **Happy can depend on how long it lasts.** The data glosses Dutch *blij* as "happy; momentarily. For generally in life, see *gelukkig*", while German *glücklich* is "happy; both momentarily and generally in life". English uses one word for both.
- **Sad and sorry can share a word.** The most common row on the sad page is Finnish *pahoillani* (#175), glossed "sorry; upset, sad". If you learn only one meaning, you'll misread the other.
- **Feelings are often verbs.** Greek χαίρομαι is "to be happy", Hungarian *örülök* "to be happy or glad", and Korean 슬퍼 and 자랑스럽다 "to be sad" and "to be proud". Korean adjectives work like verbs in general. In French and Spanish you "have" fear: *j'ai peur*, *tengo miedo*.
- **Pride can lean negative.** On the proud page, Vietnamese *kiêu* is glossed "arrogant, proud, haughty" and Basque *harrotu* "to become proud, become arrogant". Whether pride is a compliment depends on the word.
- **Some words cross into English whole.** English borrowed German *Angst* and *Schadenfreude* (pleasure at someone else's bad luck) because it had nothing quite like them. Both are in standard English dictionaries such as Merriam-Webster.

None of this means you can't talk about feelings in another language. It means the first word you learn is a starting point, and native speakers are the ones who show you where its edges are.

## Talking about feelings with a language exchange partner

Emotion words are hard to learn from a list because they only make sense attached to something that happened. A language exchange is a good place to practice them, since "how was your day?" leads straight to them.

- **Start with the everyday ones.** Tired, happy and calm come up every day; hate and fear come up less, and you'll want to hear how a native speaker softens them.
- **Tell a small true story.** "I was tired because…" or "I'm happy that…" makes you choose a word and a grammar pattern, and gives your partner something to correct.
- **Ask which word they'd really use.** If you say *glücklich* where a German would say something lighter, only a person will tell you.

## FAQ

### How do you say "happy" in different languages?

Spanish *feliz*, French *heureux*, German *glücklich*, Hebrew שמח, Arabic سعيد, Persian خوش, Turkish *mutlu* and Tagalog *masaya*. The [happy page](/tools/say/happy) has 49 languages.

### How do you say "sad" in different languages?

French and Portuguese *triste*, Czech *smutný*, Hungarian *szomorú*, Hebrew עצוב, Arabic حزين, Hindi उदास and Vietnamese *buồn*. The [sad page](/tools/say/sad) has 34 languages.

### Which emotion word is the most common across languages?

In this data, happy. Across its 49 languages the middle rank is around #400, and it is in the top 300 in languages from Persian and Urdu to Georgian, Arabic, Indonesian and Turkish. Calm and fear come next. Sad, tired and proud are much rarer, with most rows below #1,000.

### Why are some languages missing from a table?

We only include rows whose English meaning matches the feeling in the section. If a language's match on the page was a different sense, like "comfortable" for happy or "bored" for tired, we left it out rather than fill the gap from memory.
