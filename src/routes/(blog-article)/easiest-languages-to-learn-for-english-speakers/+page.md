The easiest languages for English speakers are **Spanish, Portuguese, Italian,
French, Romanian, Dutch, Norwegian, Swedish and Danish**. They form Category I
of the US Foreign Service Institute's difficulty ranking, at 24 to 30 weeks of
full-time training. Next come **German, Indonesian, Malay, Swahili and Haitian
Creole** at about 36 weeks, while Arabic, Chinese, Japanese and Korean take
about 88.

Below is what those categories mean, why each language lands where it does, and
how to choose between them. The short version: shared vocabulary, familiar
grammar and a familiar alphabet do most of the work.

## How the FSI ranks language difficulty

The Foreign Service Institute (FSI) trains US diplomats, and it publishes how
long its students usually take to reach general professional proficiency
(level 3 in speaking and listening on the Interagency Language Roundtable
scale). According to the [FSI's page on foreign language
training](https://www.state.gov/foreign-language-training/), checked in
September 2026:

| FSI category | Typical training time | Languages |
|---|---|---|
| Category I: "languages close to English" | 24 to 30 weeks (552 to 690 class hours) | Danish, Dutch, Italian, Norwegian, Portuguese, Romanian, Swedish (24 weeks); French, Spanish (30 weeks) |
| Category II | About 36 weeks (828 class hours) | German, Haitian Creole, Indonesian, Malay, Swahili |
| Category III: "hard languages" | About 44 weeks (1,012 class hours) | Including Russian, Greek, Hindi, Hebrew, Turkish, Polish, Finnish, Hungarian, Thai, Vietnamese and many more |
| Category IV: "super-hard languages" | 88 weeks (2,200 class hours) | Arabic, Chinese (Cantonese and Mandarin), Japanese, Korean |

<Callout type="info">
Read these numbers for what they are. FSI students are adults studying full
time, about 23 hours a week in class plus 17 hours of self-study, often with
earlier language experience. The FSI itself says the time varies with the
learner's ability, experience and time in class. The ranking is useful for
comparing languages, not for predicting your own timeline.
</Callout>

## Why some languages are easier for English speakers

Three things decide most of it.

**Shared vocabulary.** English is a Germanic language that borrowed an enormous
number of words from French and Latin. That gives you a head start in two
families at once: the Germanic languages (Dutch, the Scandinavian languages,
German) share everyday words like *hand*, *house* and *water* in recognizable
forms, and the Romance languages (Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese,
Romanian) share thousands of more formal words like *nation*, *possible* and
*information*.

**Familiar grammar.** Languages whose word order, verb system and noun system
resemble English feel easier. Grammatical gender, noun cases and complex verb
endings each add work.

**The same alphabet.** Every Category I and II language uses the Latin
alphabet, so you can read a word on day one. A new script is not the hardest
part of a language, but it is a real extra step, and it slows down everything
else at first.

## The easiest languages to learn, one by one

### Norwegian

Norwegian is often called the easiest of all for English speakers. Its word
order is close to English, and verbs do not change for person: "I am," "you
are" and "they are" all use the same form, *er*. Pronunciation has a musical
pitch accent and there are two written standards, but neither gets in the way of
a beginner. Start with the
[10,000 most common Norwegian words](/tools/most-common-words/norwegian).

### Swedish

Swedish is Norwegian's close neighbor, with the same simple verbs and
English-like word order, plus a few extra vowels to learn. Norwegian and
Swedish speakers can often understand each other: our overlap page lists
[118 words that are identical in Norwegian and Swedish](/tools/similar/norwegian-and-swedish)
among the two thousand each uses most. See the
[most common Swedish words](/tools/most-common-words/swedish).

### Danish

Written Danish is very close to Norwegian, with
[326 identical words in the top two thousand](/tools/similar/danish-and-norwegian).
Spoken Danish is the catch: its pronunciation is famously hard to follow, with
soft consonants and many vowel sounds. If you want to read, it is easy; if you
want to understand fast speech, give it more time. Start with the
[most common Danish words](/tools/most-common-words/danish).

### Dutch

Dutch sits between English and German. Much of its basic vocabulary will look
familiar, it has no noun cases in everyday use, and its grammar is simpler than
German's, though word order in subordinate clauses takes some getting used to,
and the throaty *g* takes practice. Knowing Dutch also opens up Afrikaans:
[153 of the most common words](/tools/similar/afrikaans-and-dutch) are the same.
See the [most common Dutch words](/tools/most-common-words/dutch).

### Spanish

Spanish is the most popular choice on this list for good reason. Spelling is
consistent, so you can pronounce almost any word you read; it has only five
vowel sounds; and it shares a large Latin vocabulary with English. The FSI puts
it at 30 weeks rather than 24 without giving a reason; for most learners the
hard part is the verb system, with many tenses and the subjunctive. It is also spoken in a huge number of countries,
which makes finding practice partners easy. Start with the
[10,000 most common Spanish words](/tools/most-common-words/spanish) and see how
many you know with the [Spanish vocabulary test](/tools/vocabulary-test/spanish).

### Portuguese

Portuguese grammar is close to Spanish, and its vocabulary overlaps heavily:
[167 of the most common words are written the same](/tools/similar/portuguese-and-spanish),
and many more differ by a letter or two. Pronunciation is harder than Spanish,
with nasal vowels and reduced syllables, and Brazilian and European Portuguese
sound quite different. See the
[most common Portuguese words](/tools/most-common-words/portuguese).

### Italian

Italian is spelled almost exactly as it sounds and shares a lot of vocabulary
with English through Latin, and with Spanish directly
([44 identical words among the most common](/tools/similar/italian-and-spanish),
and far more near-identical ones). Its verbs conjugate a lot, which is the main
work. Start with the [most common Italian words](/tools/most-common-words/italian).

### French

French has more shared vocabulary with English than any other language on this
list, thanks to centuries of borrowing after 1066. The hard part, and a likely
reason it is rated at 30 weeks, is the gap between spelling and sound: silent letters, linked words
and nasal vowels mean that reading French is much easier than understanding it
at speed. See the [10,000 most common French words](/tools/most-common-words/french)
or take the [French vocabulary test](/tools/vocabulary-test/french).

### Romanian

Romanian is the Romance language people forget. It keeps a lot of Latin
vocabulary, which makes it more familiar than it looks, and it also borrowed
words from Slavic languages, Hungarian and Turkish. It has some grammar the
others do not, including noun cases and a definite article attached to the end
of a noun. The FSI still puts it in Category I. See the
[most common Romanian words](/tools/most-common-words/romanian).

### Indonesian and Malay

These are the easiest Asian languages for English speakers, both in Category II.
They use the Latin alphabet, verbs do not change for tense or person, there is no
grammatical gender, and plurals are often made by simply repeating a word. The
challenge is mostly vocabulary, which has little in common with English, and
prefixes and suffixes that shift a word's meaning. The two are closely
related: [372 of the most common words are identical](/tools/similar/indonesian-and-malay).
See the [most common Indonesian words](/tools/most-common-words/indonesian).

### German

German shares much of its core vocabulary with English, but it has three
grammatical genders and four cases, which change the form of articles, adjectives
and nouns. That is a big reason German is usually rated harder than Dutch.
The payoff is a language with a regular, logical structure once it clicks. See
the [10,000 most common German words](/tools/most-common-words/german).

### Honorable mentions: Afrikaans and Esperanto

Neither appears on the FSI list. **Afrikaans** developed from Dutch and is
often described as one of the simplest Germanic languages, with verbs that
barely change and no grammatical gender. **Esperanto** is a constructed language
designed to be regular, with no irregular verbs. Both have free word lists:
[Afrikaans](/tools/most-common-words/afrikaans) and
[Esperanto](/tools/most-common-words/esperanto).

## The hardest languages for English speakers

Category IV, the FSI's "super-hard" group at 88 weeks, is **Arabic, Mandarin,
Cantonese, Japanese and Korean**. The reasons stack up:

- **Scripts.** Chinese uses thousands of characters; Japanese mixes Chinese
  characters with two syllabaries; Arabic runs right to left and usually leaves
  short vowels unwritten.
- **Sounds.** Mandarin and Cantonese are tonal: the same syllable means
  different things depending on pitch.
- **Grammar.** Japanese and Korean put the verb at the end and encode politeness
  levels in the grammar. Arabic builds words from consonant roots, and the
  spoken dialects differ considerably from the standard written language.
- **Vocabulary.** Apart from loanwords, very little is shared with English.

The encouraging part is that not every piece is hard. The Korean alphabet has
[just 24 letters](/tools/alphabet/korean) and can be learned in a few days of study, and
Chinese has no verb conjugation at all. Our frequency lists for
[Chinese](/tools/most-common-words/chinese),
[Korean](/tools/most-common-words/korean) and
[Arabic](/tools/most-common-words/arabic) are a good way to see what
you are signing up for, and the [Arabic alphabet](/tools/alphabet/arabic) page
shows every letter with its sound.

Category III sits between the two, including
[Russian](/tools/alphabet/russian), [Greek](/tools/alphabet/greek),
[Hindi](/tools/alphabet/hindi) and Turkish. Most of them combine a
new script or complex grammar with limited shared vocabulary.

## What actually makes a language easy for you

The FSI measures one thing: time for a native English speaker to reach
general professional proficiency in a classroom. Your own experience will depend on a
few things the ranking cannot see:

- **The languages you already know.** If you speak Spanish, Portuguese and
  Italian get much cheaper; our [language overlap](/tools/similar) pages show
  exactly how many common words carry over between 55 pairs.
- **Motivation.** A "hard" language you care about beats an "easy" one you do
  not. People who love K-dramas learn Korean.
- **Access to speakers.** The easiest language to learn is one you can
  practice every day. With Spanish, French or Portuguese, you will never run out
  of partners; with Danish or Romanian, you will have to look a bit harder.

## FAQ

### What is the easiest language for English speakers to learn?

According to the FSI, the Category I languages take the least time: Danish,
Dutch, Italian, Norwegian, Portuguese, Romanian and Swedish at 24 weeks, and
Spanish and French at 30. Many learners find Norwegian, Dutch or Spanish the
easiest to start with.

### Is Spanish or French easier for English speakers?

The FSI puts both at 30 weeks. French shares more vocabulary with English, but
its spelling and pronunciation are harder to match up; Spanish is spelled as it
sounds, which makes speaking and listening easier early on. Most learners find
Spanish easier to get started with.

### How long does it take to learn Spanish?

The FSI estimates 30 weeks of full-time study, 23 hours of class a week plus
self-study, to reach general professional proficiency. Part-time learners take
longer; how much longer depends on how many hours a week you put in and how
much you speak.

### What is the easiest Asian language to learn?

Indonesian and Malay, both FSI Category II. They use the Latin alphabet and have
no verb conjugation for tense or person and no grammatical gender.

### What is the hardest language for English speakers?

The FSI's Category IV: Arabic, Mandarin, Cantonese, Japanese and Korean, each at
about 88 weeks of full-time study, more than three times as long as the 24-week languages.
