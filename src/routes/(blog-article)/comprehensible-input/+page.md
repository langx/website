Comprehensible input is language you can understand, in reading or listening,
even though it contains a few things you don't know yet. The idea comes from the
linguist Stephen Krashen, who argued that we acquire a language by understanding
messages in it, not by studying its rules. To use it, you find books, podcasts
and videos pitched just above your level and consume a lot of them, then add
speaking and writing, because input alone leaves gaps.

That last part is where much of the online advice goes wrong. This guide covers
what Krashen actually claimed, what later research supports and what it
doesn't, how to find input at the right level, and how conversation fits in.

## What is comprehensible input?

Stephen Krashen set out his theory of second language acquisition in the late
1970s and early 1980s, most fully in *Principles and Practice in Second Language
Acquisition* (1982) and *The Input Hypothesis* (1985). It rests on five linked
hypotheses:

- **Acquisition vs learning.** Acquisition is the subconscious process that
  builds real ability; learning is conscious knowledge of rules.
- **The monitor.** Learned rules only act as an editor that checks your output
  when you have time to think.
- **Natural order.** Grammar features tend to be acquired in a predictable
  order, whatever order they are taught in.
- **Input.** We acquire language by understanding input slightly beyond our
  current level, which Krashen wrote as **i+1**.
- **Affective filter.** Anxiety, low motivation and low confidence block input
  from being absorbed.

The input hypothesis is the heart of it. If your current level is "i", the input
that moves you forward is "i+1": mostly familiar, with a little that is new, and
enough context that you can work out the new part. Krashen's practical advice
follows directly: read and listen to large amounts of interesting material you
can understand, and don't force speech before learners are ready.

## What does the evidence say about comprehensible input?

Krashen's ideas shaped language teaching for decades, and they have also been
argued over for decades. Here is a fair summary.

| Claim | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| You need large amounts of understandable input | Widely accepted. Extensive reading and listening research supports it, and no serious approach works without input. |
| Input should be mostly understood | Supported. Vocabulary research on lexical coverage (below) gives useful numbers. |
| Anxiety interferes with learning | Broadly consistent with later research on language anxiety, though the "filter" itself is a metaphor. |
| Input alone is sufficient | Contested. Studies of immersion students and interaction research point to roles for output and feedback. |
| Conscious study of grammar doesn't help acquisition | Contested. Research on noticing and explicit instruction suggests attention to form can help. |
| The theory can be tested as stated | Criticized. Barry McLaughlin (1987) argued key terms like "acquisition" and "i+1" were too vague to test. |

The most influential challenge came from Merrill Swain. Studying French
immersion students in Canada, who had received years of comprehensible input in
school, she found they understood French very well but their speaking and
writing remained noticeably less native-like. Her **output hypothesis** (1985)
argued that producing language pushes you to process grammar more deeply than
understanding does. When you try to say something, you notice the gap between
what you want to say and what you can say, you test your guesses about how the
language works, and you get feedback on them.

Two other ideas round out the picture. Michael Long's **interaction hypothesis**
holds that conversation helps because speakers negotiate meaning: they ask for
clarification, rephrase and check understanding, which turns hard input into
comprehensible input on the spot. Richard Schmidt's **noticing hypothesis**
(1990) argued that you learn what you pay attention to, which gives a role to
consciously noticing forms rather than just absorbing meaning.

A balanced reading, reflected in Paul Nation's well-known "four strands"
framework, is that a good program needs meaning-focused input, meaning-focused
output, deliberate language study and fluency practice, in roughly equal
measure. Input is necessary. It is rarely enough on its own.

## How do you find input at your level?

"Slightly above your level" sounds vague, but vocabulary research gives you a
concrete test. Researchers measure **lexical coverage**, the share of words in a
text that you know. Hu and Nation (2000) found that around 98% coverage was
needed for most learners to read fiction without help, and other studies treat
95% as the level for reasonable but less complete understanding.

In practice, that means:

- **If you are looking up a word in every line, it is too hard.** One unknown
  word in twenty is already a lot in a fast conversation.
- **If you understand everything and learn nothing, it is too easy.** Useful as
  fluency practice, but move up soon.
- **You should be able to follow the story without stopping.** Pausing to guess
  a word from context is fine; pausing to decode every sentence is not.

Our post on [how many words you need to be
fluent](/how-many-words-do-you-need-to-be-fluent) explains the coverage research
in detail. To see roughly where you stand, try a free [vocabulary
test](/tools/vocabulary-test/spanish) (it exists for [French](/tools/vocabulary-test/french),
[German](/tools/vocabulary-test/german) and dozens of other languages), and if
your input keeps feeling too hard, learn the [most common
words](/tools/most-common-words) first so each text has fewer gaps.

## Where to find comprehensible input: graded readers, podcasts and more

Good input is interesting, at the right level, and plentiful. The best source
changes as you improve.

| Level | Type of input | Examples (at the time of writing, September 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Beginner | Comprehensible input videos with gestures and drawings | [Dreaming Spanish](https://www.dreaming.com/spanish) superbeginner videos |
| Beginner | Graded readers | Oxford Bookworms and Penguin Readers for English; beginner short story collections for many other languages |
| Beginner to intermediate | Learner podcasts with transcripts | [Coffee Break Languages](https://coffeebreaklanguages.com), podcasts made for learners in your language |
| Intermediate | Street interviews with subtitles | The Easy Languages channels on YouTube (Easy German, Easy Spanish and others) |
| Intermediate | Reading with a click-to-look-up tool | [LingQ](https://www.lingq.com), a reading and listening platform built around input |
| Intermediate to advanced | Native TV and video with dual subtitles | Streaming shows with a subtitle extension such as [Language Reactor](https://www.languagereactor.com) |
| Advanced | Native books, news and podcasts | Anything you would read or listen to in your own language |

**Graded readers** deserve a special mention. They are books written with a
controlled vocabulary for each level, which makes it easy to hit 98% coverage
and read quickly. Reading many easy books is usually better than struggling
through one hard one.

**Narrow input** also helps. Krashen recommended "narrow reading" and "narrow
listening": sticking to one topic, author or show for a while. The same words
and situations come back again and again, so each new text is easier than the
last.

## A simple daily comprehensible input routine

You don't need hours a day. What matters is regular, understandable input and a
way to keep the words you meet.

## The output side: why you still need to speak

Swain's point applies to almost every self-learner. You can understand podcasts
comfortably and still freeze when someone asks you a simple question, because
understanding and producing are different skills. Output does things input
can't:

- **It shows you your gaps.** You only discover you don't know how to say "I was
  going to" when you try.
- **It tests your guesses.** Each sentence you produce is a small experiment.
  Feedback tells you if it worked.
- **It builds speed.** Retrieving words under time pressure is a skill that only
  improves with practice.

Speaking before you are ready can be uncomfortable, which is Krashen's affective
filter at work. The answer is not to avoid output, but to find low-pressure
output: writing messages instead of speaking live, talking to yourself (see [how
to think in a foreign language](/how-to-think-in-a-foreign-language)), and
practising with one patient partner rather than a crowd.

## Conversation is comprehensible input and output at once

A conversation with a native speaker who knows you are learning is the best of
both worlds. Their messages are input tuned to your level, because they adapt
to you, and you can ask what something means, which is exactly the negotiation
of meaning Long described. Your replies are output, and their corrections are
the feedback Swain and Schmidt highlighted.

## FAQ

### What is comprehensible input in simple terms?

It is reading or listening material you mostly understand, with a few new words
or structures you can work out from context. Stephen Krashen argued that this
kind of input is what drives language acquisition.

### What does i+1 mean?

In Krashen's input hypothesis, "i" is your current level and "i+1" is input that
is one step beyond it: mostly familiar, with a small amount of new language you
can understand from context.

### Is comprehensible input enough to become fluent?

For most people, no. Input is essential and should be most of your time, but
research by Merrill Swain on immersion students showed that people who only get
input understand far better than they speak. You also need to speak and write,
and get feedback.

### How much comprehensible input do I need per day?

There is no proven number. Regular daily input, such as 30 to 60 minutes, is a
realistic target for busy learners, and more helps if you enjoy it.

### Can beginners use comprehensible input?

Yes. Beginner comprehensible input videos use gestures, drawings and very simple
speech, and graded readers start with a few hundred words. Pair them with
learning the most common words so your input becomes understandable faster.
