To improve your speaking in a foreign language, you have to **produce the
language out loud, regularly, and get feedback on it**. Listening and reading
build understanding, but speaking is its own skill. The fastest ways to train it
without moving abroad are shadowing native audio, recording and reviewing
yourself, sending voice messages to native speakers, and having someone correct
what you say.

Most learners understand far more than they can say. That gap is normal, and it
closes only with output practice. Below are the methods that work, what each one
trains, and a 30-day plan that puts them together.

## Why speaking lags behind everything else

Understanding a sentence and building one are different jobs. When you listen,
context fills the gaps. When you speak, you have to retrieve the words, put them
in order, conjugate them and pronounce them, all in real time.

The linguist Merrill Swain made this point in her "output hypothesis" (1985):
producing language forces you to notice what you don't know yet, in a way that
comprehension alone does not. You find out that you can't remember the past
tense of a verb only when you need it mid-sentence.

That is why living abroad helps. It isn't the air, it's the daily, unavoidable
speaking. You can recreate most of that at home if you build the speaking in on
purpose.

## The speaking methods at a glance

| Method | What it trains | Needs a partner? | Time per day |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shadowing | Pronunciation, rhythm, speed | No | 10 to 15 min |
| Talking to yourself | Retrieval, fluency | No | 5 to 10 min |
| Recording yourself | Self-monitoring, accuracy | No | 5 to 10 min |
| Voice messages | Real communication, low pressure | Yes | 10 to 20 min |
| Live conversation | Spontaneity, listening under pressure | Yes | 20 to 60 min |
| Getting corrections | Accuracy, fixing fossilized errors | Yes | Built into the above |

The solo methods build the engine; the partner methods test it on a real road.
You need both.

## 1. Shadowing: repeat native speech in real time

Shadowing means playing a short native recording and speaking along with it, a
fraction of a second behind, copying the rhythm, stress and intonation as
closely as you can. It comes from interpreter training and was popularized among
self-learners by polyglot Alexander Arguelles.

How to do it well:

- **Pick short audio with a transcript**: a podcast for learners, an audiobook
  chapter, or dialogue from a show with subtitles.
- **Listen once, read once, then shadow.** Don't shadow material you don't
  understand yet.
- **Loop one to two minutes** for ten minutes rather than covering a lot of
  ground.
- **Exaggerate the melody.** Most accents come from rhythm and intonation more
  than individual sounds.

If you need to hear how a single word is said, our [word pronunciation
tool](/tools/say) plays everyday words like [hello](/tools/say/hello) across
many languages.

## 2. Talk to yourself, out loud

It sounds silly. It works because it is free, private and unlimited. Describe
what you are doing while you cook. Explain your plans for tomorrow. Retell a
video you just watched.

When you hit a word you don't know, note it and look it up afterward. That list
of gaps is gold: it is exactly the vocabulary you need and don't have. Our guide
on [how to think in a foreign language](/how-to-think-in-a-foreign-language)
turns this into a daily habit.

Introverts often find this the easiest place to start; see our [introvert's
guide to learning a language](/mastering-a-new-language-an-introverts-guide) for
more low-pressure ideas.

## 3. Record yourself and listen back

Recording yourself is uncomfortable and very effective. You cannot hear your own
mistakes while you are busy producing the sentence; on playback, you can.

A simple routine:

1. Pick a prompt, such as "describe your weekend" or "explain your job."
2. Speak for one to two minutes without stopping.
3. Listen back once for **pronunciation** and once for **grammar**.
4. Write down three things to fix, then record again.

Keep your first recording. In three months, playing it next to a new one is the
best motivation you will find.

## 4. Send voice messages to native speakers

Voice messages are the bridge between solo practice and live conversation. You
speak to a real person, but you can prepare, re-record and send when you are
ready. For many learners, that removes most of the anxiety that stops them from
speaking at all.

## 5. Have real conversations

Nothing replaces a conversation where you can't predict what comes next. That
is where you learn to keep going when you don't know a word: rephrase, describe
it, use a simpler structure.

Where to find them without moving abroad:

- **A language exchange partner.** You help with your language, they help with
  theirs. Our guides on [how to find a language exchange
  partner](/how-to-find-a-language-exchange-partner) and [how to do a language
  exchange](/how-to-do-a-language-exchange) cover the details.
- **A paid tutor.** Platforms such as [italki](https://www.italki.com) and
  [Preply](https://preply.com) connect you with teachers for live video
  lessons. A tutor is the most structured option and the easiest way to get
  guaranteed speaking time.
- **Local meetups.** Many cities have language cafes and conversation groups.

Run out of things to say? Keep a list of [conversation
topics](/language-exchange-conversation-topics) and prepare two or three before
each session.

## 6. Get corrected, and ask for it

Speaking a lot without feedback can lock in mistakes. Learners who have been
speaking for years without corrections often have errors that feel "right" to
them because they have repeated them so many times.

Tell your partner or tutor how you want to be corrected. Some people like every
error pointed out; others prefer one or two key corrections per conversation.
In a text or voice exchange, a written correction you can keep is especially
useful, because you can review it later.

On LangX, you can hold any message to correct it, and corrections are
unlimited on every plan, including Free. A practical trick: after sending a
voice note, type out what you said and ask your partner to correct the text.

## 7. Work on pronunciation directly

Pronunciation is partly a physical skill. A few targeted habits help:

- **Learn the sounds that don't exist in your language.** Most languages have
  three to five sounds that give learners away. Find yours and drill them.
- **Use minimal pairs.** Practice word pairs that differ by one sound, such as
  the Spanish *pero* and *perro*.
- **Learn the writing system properly.** If your target language uses a new
  script, a solid grasp of it helps you connect spelling and sound. Our
  [alphabet guides](/tools/alphabet/korean) are a quick way in.
- **Copy whole phrases, not just words.** Stress and linking between words
  matter as much as individual sounds.

## 8. Build the words you need to speak

You can't say what you don't know. The core spoken vocabulary of most languages
is surprisingly small; our article on [how many words you need to be
fluent](/how-many-words-do-you-need-to-be-fluent) explains how far the first
couple of thousand words take you. Start with a [most common words
list](/tools/most-common-words) and make sure you can *say* those words, not
just recognize them.

Learn words in chunks: "I'd like to," "the thing is," "what I mean is." Chunks
are what make speech sound fluent, and they buy you thinking time mid-sentence.

## A 30-day plan to improve your speaking

This plan assumes about 30 minutes a day. Adjust the times to your schedule, but
keep something every day.

| Week | Daily routine | Weekly goal |
|---|---|---|
| Week 1: warm up | 10 min shadowing, 5 min talking to yourself, 1 short recording | Find 2 or 3 exchange partners and send your first voice message |
| Week 2: speak to people | 10 min shadowing, 3 to 5 voice messages to partners, note every correction | Record a 2-minute self-introduction and compare it with day 1 |
| Week 3: go longer | 10 min shadowing, one longer voice message or conversation, review corrections | Have at least one live conversation (tutor, meetup or friend) |
| Week 4: fix and stretch | 10 min drilling your top 5 recurring errors, conversations on new topics | Record the same prompt as week 1 and listen to both |

<Callout type="success">
At the end of the month, you should have a list of your most frequent mistakes,
a handful of regular partners, and two recordings that show the difference. That
list of mistakes is what month two is built on.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes that slow speaking down

- **Waiting until you're "ready."** You won't feel ready. Start with voice notes.
- **Only practicing with apps that give you the words.** Choosing between four
  options is not the same as producing a sentence. See our list of [Duolingo
  alternatives for speaking](/duolingo-alternatives-for-speaking) for options
  that make you talk.
- **Translating everything in your head first.** Aim for short, simple sentences
  you can build directly.
- **Avoiding corrections.** Mistakes are information. Ask for them.
- **Stopping after a bad day.** Some days will feel worse than last week. That's
  normal; consistency wins over months, as we explain in [why consistent
  practice is key](/why-consistent-practice-is-key-to-language-learning-success).

## FAQ

### How can I practice speaking a foreign language alone?

Shadow native audio, talk to yourself about what you're doing, and record
yourself answering prompts, then listen back. Solo practice builds fluency, but
add a partner or tutor for feedback.

### How long does it take to improve speaking skills?

Most learners notice a real difference within a few weeks of daily speaking
practice, especially in confidence. Reaching a comfortable conversational level
takes months to years depending on the language; see [how long it takes to
learn a language](/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-a-language).

### Are voice messages as good as live conversation?

They train different things. Voice messages let you prepare and reduce anxiety;
live conversation trains speed and spontaneity. Start with voice messages and
add live conversation as your confidence grows.

### How do I stop freezing when I speak?

Prepare a few topics in advance, learn filler phrases that buy you time, and
lower the stakes with voice messages before live conversation. Freezing gets rarer the
more often you speak.

### Is shadowing effective?

Many learners and teachers find it useful for pronunciation and rhythm, and it
is easy to do daily. It does not replace real conversation, because you are
repeating someone else's sentences rather than building your own.
