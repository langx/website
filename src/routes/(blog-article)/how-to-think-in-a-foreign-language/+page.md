To think in a foreign language, **use it for the small, private moments of your
day**: narrate what you're doing, name the objects around you, rehearse
conversations in your head, and write a few sentences each evening. Start with
simple sentences you can build without translating, and make it a daily habit.
Over weeks, the language starts showing up on its own.

Thinking in the language is less a trick than a side effect of lots of use. The
nine habits below give you that use in minutes a day, without a classroom or a
partner. First, an honest look at what is and isn't known about it.

## What the evidence does and doesn't show

It is worth being clear, because a lot of advice online overstates this.

**What is documented:**

- **Learners do develop inner speech in a second language.** María de Guerrero's
  book *Inner Speech – L2: Thinking Words in a Second Language* (Springer, 2005)
  reviews research on how learners come to use a new language silently, for
  rehearsing, planning and talking to themselves.
- **The "din in the head" is a real, described experience.** Many learners
  report words and phrases from a new language replaying involuntarily after
  intensive exposure. It was described by Barber (1980) and discussed by
  Stephen Krashen in "The Din in the Head, Input, and the Language Acquisition
  Device" (1983), who linked it to large amounts of understandable input.
- **Thinking in a foreign language can change how you decide.** Keysar,
  Hayakawa and An (2012, *Psychological Science*) found that people facing
  choices framed in a foreign language showed smaller framing effects and less
  loss aversion than in their native language.

**What is not shown:**

- There is no strong controlled evidence that deliberately "forcing" yourself
  to think in a language makes you learn it faster than other practice of the
  same length. The habits below are well-reasoned and widely recommended, but
  they are practice routines, not proven shortcuts.
- The decision-making research above is about judgment, not about learning. It
  says nothing about whether inner narration improves your grammar.

<Callout type="info">
The sensible reading: inner speech in a new language grows with use, and these
habits are cheap ways to get more use. Treat them as extra practice minutes that
fit into the gaps of your day, not as a secret method.
</Callout>

## 1. Narrate what you're doing

Describe your actions in simple present-tense sentences as you do them. "I'm
opening the fridge. There's no milk. I need to buy milk." It feels basic, and
that's the point: these are the sentences you actually need.

Do it during one routine, such as making breakfast or your commute, so it
becomes automatic. When you hit a gap, don't stop to look it up; note it and
check later. Our [most common words lists](/tools/most-common-words), such as
[French](/tools/most-common-words/french), are a good source for the verbs and
nouns that come up most.

## 2. Label the world around you

As you walk through a room or down a street, name what you see: table, window,
bus, tree. At first, you'll only have a few words. That's useful information:
the objects you can't name are the vocabulary gaps in your daily life.

Some learners put sticky notes on objects at home. That works well for the
first few hundred words. After that, move on to naming and adding a detail:
"a red car," "an empty cup."

## 3. Think in chunks, not single words

Fluent speakers don't assemble sentences word by word; they use ready-made
phrases. "Let me think," "it depends," "I'm not sure," "the problem is." Collect
these chunks and use them in your inner narration. They make thinking in the
language faster, because a single retrieval gives you a whole piece of a
sentence.

A handy way to find them: note phrases your conversation partners repeat, then
adopt them.

## 4. Describe instead of translating

The urge to translate comes from wanting to say something complex that you
don't have the words for. The fix is to simplify. If you don't know the word
for "ladder," think "the thing you climb to reach high places."

This skill, called circumlocution, is what fluent speakers do all the time in
their second language. Practicing it in your head trains you to keep going in
the language instead of switching back to your own. Games that make you guess
from definitions help too; try the [meaning quiz](/tools/meaning-quiz) or the
[word game](/tools/word-game).

## 5. Talk to yourself out loud

Silent thought lets you skip words and gloss over grammar. Saying it out loud
exposes the gaps. Five minutes of talking to yourself, while walking or doing
the dishes, is some of the most efficient practice there is.

If you want to go further with this, our guide on [how to improve your speaking
in a foreign language](/how-to-improve-speaking-skills-in-a-foreign-language)
covers shadowing and recording yourself.

## 6. Keep a three-sentence journal

Each evening, write three sentences about your day in the language: one thing
that happened, one thing you felt, one thing you plan to do tomorrow. Three is
small enough that you won't skip it and big enough to use past, present and
future.

Writing is slower than thinking, which is exactly why it helps: you have time to
notice what you're unsure about. Once a week, get a native speaker to correct a
few entries.

## 7. Switch your phone's language

Changing your phone, browser or favorite app to your target language gives you
hundreds of small exposures a day to words like "settings," "send," "delete"
and "search."

Honest caveat: you'll learn interface vocabulary quickly, and then the benefit
levels off, because you stop reading the labels once you know where things are.
It's a good nudge, not a method. Only do it once you're comfortable enough to
find your way back.

## 8. Rehearse and replay conversations

Before a conversation, run through it in your head in the target language: how
you'll greet the person, what you'll ask, how you'll answer the obvious
questions. After it, replay the moments where you got stuck and work out what
you could have said.

This is the habit that connects inner speech to real speaking. It is also
exactly how the "din" tends to show up for many learners: phrases from a recent
conversation keep replaying on their own.

For prepared material, keep a list of [conversation
topics](/language-exchange-conversation-topics) and pick one to rehearse each
day.

## 9. Chat with a native speaker every day

Inner speech runs out of material if you never get new input. A daily exchange
with a native speaker feeds your thinking with real phrases, and it gives you
somebody to check the sentences you've been building in your head.

## How long until you think in the language?

It depends on how much you use it. Many learners notice brief moments of
thinking in the language, a phrase that comes to mind without translating,
somewhere around a lower-intermediate level, once the most common words are
automatic. Longer stretches of inner speech tend to come with more exposure and
conversation.

There's no published timeline you can rely on, but the general picture of how
long languages take is covered in our guide to [how long it takes to learn a
language](/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-a-language). Vocabulary matters too:
see [how many words you need to be fluent](/how-many-words-do-you-need-to-be-fluent).

## FAQ

### Is it possible to think in a foreign language?

Yes. Research on second-language inner speech, such as de Guerrero (2005),
shows that learners develop it, and many learners describe phrases replaying in
their heads after intensive exposure. It grows with use rather than appearing
at a fixed level.

### How do I stop translating in my head?

Use simpler sentences you can build directly, learn common phrases as whole
chunks, and describe unknown words instead of reaching for a translation.
Translating less is a gradual process, not a switch.

### Does thinking in a foreign language make you fluent faster?

There's no strong evidence that it does on its own. It adds practice minutes
and builds retrieval speed, which help, but it works best alongside
vocabulary, listening and real conversation.

### Can beginners think in a foreign language?

Yes, in a limited way. Beginners can narrate simple actions and label objects
with a few hundred words. Keep sentences short and don't worry about mistakes.

### Is it normal to dream in a foreign language?

Many learners report it, often after periods of heavy use. It's a common
experience, but it isn't a reliable sign of level, and not dreaming in the
language doesn't mean you aren't progressing.
