## How to pick an app to practice French

**Decide which French you need, roughly.** French is spoken in France, Belgium, Switzerland, Quebec and across West and Central Africa, and speakers understand each other easily. The differences are real, though:

- **Quebec French** has its own accent and everyday words: a Quebecer may say *magasiner* for "to go shopping" and *char* for "car", and a *blonde* is a girlfriend, not only a blonde woman.
- **Belgian and Swiss French** say *septante* (70) and *nonante* (90) where France says *soixante-dix* and *quatre-vingt-dix*.
- **France's French** is what most textbooks teach, so it is the easiest match for course material.

If you are moving to Montreal or working with a Belgian team, filter partners by country. If not, talk to several; the variety trains your ear.

**Plan for listening, not only talking.** Spoken French drops sounds that written French keeps. People say *je sais pas* instead of *je ne sais pas*, use *on* where textbooks use *nous*, and run words together through liaison. Apps with voice messages or calls matter more for French than for a language spelled the way it sounds.

**Match the format to your level.** Beginners get more from text and voice messages, with time to look things up. If you already read well but freeze in conversation, you need live speaking: calls with a partner or a tutor.

**Be ready to give back.** Your partner will want help with your language, very often English, so English speakers usually find partners quickly.

## Language exchange apps for French

### HelloTalk

[HelloTalk](https://www.hellotalk.com) is the largest exchange community, so French speakers are easy to find. Its **Moments** feed suits French well: post a few sentences about your week, and native speakers correct your genders, agreements and tenses.

- **Best for:** finding partners quickly and seeing several corrections of the same sentence.
- **Keep in mind:** the free tier has ads and daily caps on translation and some tools; VIP removes them. Popular French profiles get many messages, so a specific opener helps.

## French-specific resources worth adding

**[TV5MONDE EDU](https://apprendre.tv5monde.com)**, from the international French-language broadcaster, is free and built on real video: news reports, music videos and film clips with exercises, from A1 to B2. It pairs well with a partner who can explain what you did not catch.

**[Dreaming](https://www.dreaming.com)**, known for Dreaming Spanish, now teaches French too, with graded videos of native speakers from "superbeginner" up. It builds listening through comprehensible input and has a free tier and a paid one.

## Tu or vous in a French language exchange

- **Vous is the polite default** with strangers, older people and anyone in a formal setting.
- **Tu is normal between exchange partners of similar age.** Many apps have a casual tone, and most partners will use tu from the first message.
- **If you are unsure, ask.** *On se tutoie ?* ("Shall we say tu?") is a standard, friendly question, and asking it is itself good practice.
- **Vous is also plural.** Talking to two people, even friends, you use vous.

## Your first message in French

Keep it short, friendly and specific to their profile.

> Bonjour Camille ! Je m'appelle Sam et je viens du Canada. J'apprends le français et j'ai vu que tu apprends l'anglais. Je t'aide en anglais et tu corriges mon français ?

In English: "Hello Camille! My name is Sam and I'm from Canada. I'm learning French and I saw you're learning English. I help you with English and you correct my French?" (The space before "!" and "?" is French typography as used in France; Quebec usually leaves it out.)

Three more phrases you will use every week:

- **Comment dit-on... en français ?** How do you say... in French?
- **Est-ce que ça se dit ?** Is that something people say?
- **Plus lentement, s'il te plaît.** More slowly, please. (Use *s'il vous plaît* with vous.)

## Tips for French learners on exchange apps

**Ask for corrections on the things you do not notice.** Noun gender (*le* or *la*), adjective agreement, and the choice between passé composé and imparfait are the classic learner mistakes. Name them in your profile or first message and partners will watch for them.

**Set up a French keyboard.** Typing é, è, à, ç and ê properly takes seconds and makes you easier to read. A missing accent can change a word: *ou* means "or", *où* means "where".

**Send voice messages from the first week.** French spelling does not tell you how to pronounce a word, so you need someone to hear you. Ask your partner to reply with a voice message too, and listen before reading.

**Learn the spoken shortcuts.** When your partner writes *chuis* for *je suis* or leaves out *ne*, that is normal informal French, not a mistake. Ask what is casual and what is standard.

## FAQ

### What is the best free app to practice French with native speakers?

HelloTalk and Tandem have the most French speakers and free tiers with ads and caps. Speaky asks for no subscription. LangX has no ads and unlimited corrections, with 5 new conversations a day on Free. For free listening practice, TV5MONDE EDU is excellent.

### Should I learn French from France or Quebec?

Pick the one you are most likely to use. Most courses teach France's French, and Quebecers understand it perfectly. If you are moving to Quebec, find partners and a tutor from there early, because the accent takes some getting used to.

### Should I use tu or vous with a language exchange partner?

Tu is normal between exchange partners of similar age. Use vous with someone much older or in a formal setting, and if you are unsure, ask "On se tutoie ?"

### Can I practice French speaking for free?

Yes. HelloTalk and Tandem include free calls with partners, and Conversation Exchange helps you meet someone in person. LangX supports voice messages but not live calls. Tutors on italki and Preply are paid.
