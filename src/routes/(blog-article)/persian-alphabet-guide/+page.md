The Persian alphabet, also called the Farsi alphabet, has 32 letters. It is the Arabic script with four letters added for sounds Arabic doesn't have: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">پ</span> (p), <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">چ</span> (ch), <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ژ</span> (zh) and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">گ</span> (g). It is written right to left, most letters join to the next one, and the short vowels are usually left out, so reading takes some practice even after you know every letter.

This guide takes the letters, names and sounds from our free [Persian alphabet chart](/tools/alphabet/persian), and the practice words come from our list of the [most common Persian words](/tools/most-common-words/persian), with their ranks.

## What kind of script is Persian?

Persian is an Indo-European language, related to English, Hindi and Russian, written in a script borrowed from a Semitic language. After the Arab conquest of Iran in the 7th century, Persian came to be written in the Arabic script, and it has been for more than a thousand years. Iran and Afghanistan (where the language is called Dari) use this script; Tajik, the form of Persian spoken in Tajikistan, is written in Cyrillic.

The script is an **abjad**: consonants are full letters, and vowels are only partly written. The long vowels have letters. The short vowels have optional marks above and below the line that everyday text leaves out. A Persian reader supplies them from knowing the word, which is why a beginner needs both the letters and some vocabulary.

Because the alphabet came from Arabic, a learner of either language gets a head start on the other. Our [Arabic alphabet guide](/arabic-alphabet-guide) covers the original 28 letters, and our [Arabic and Persian word comparison](/tools/similar/arabic-and-persian) lists common words the two languages share.

The new letters were made by adding dots or a stroke to Arabic ones: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">پ</span> is <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ب</span> with three dots, <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">چ</span> is <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ج</span> with three, <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ژ</span> is <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ز</span> with three, and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">گ</span> is <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ک</span> with a second stroke. Persian words use them constantly: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">پدر</span> (pedar, father, #151), <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">چرا</span> (cherâ, why, #35), <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">گربه</span> (gorbe, cat, #712). <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ژ</span> is the rare one; you'll meet it in loanwords such as <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ژاکت</span> (jacket, #2,448).

Two letters also look slightly different from their Arabic versions. Persian writes kaf as <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ک</span>, where Arabic usually has <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ك</span>, and Persian ye has no dots at the end of a word (<span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ی</span>), where Arabic ya has two (<span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ي</span>). ## Letters that share one sound

Arabic has sounds Persian does not, so several letters that are distinct in Arabic are said exactly alike in Persian. For reading this is no problem. For spelling, you learn which letter each word uses.

- **z** is written four ways. <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ز</span> is the usual one, as in <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">زن</span> (zan, woman, #163). <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ذ</span> is in <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">غذا</span> (ghazâ, food, #325), <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ض</span> in <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ضعیف</span> (za'if, weak, #2,270), and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ظ</span> in <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ظهر</span> (zohr, noon, #1,086).
- **s** is written three ways: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">س</span> in <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">سلام</span> (salâm, hello, #60), <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ث</span> in <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ثانیه</span> (sâniye, second, #1,309), <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ص</span> in <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">صبح</span> (sobh, morning, #198).
- **t** is written two ways: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ت</span> in <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">تو</span> (to, you, #6) and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ط</span> in <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">طلا</span> (talâ, gold, #1,099).
- **h** is written two ways: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ه</span> in <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">هوا</span> (havâ, air, #511) and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ح</span> in <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">حال</span> (hâl, state, #112). To tell them apart, Persians call <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ح</span> "he-ye jimi" (the he shaped like jim) and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ه</span> "he-ye do-cheshm" (the two-eyed he).

The less common letters in each group (<span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ث ح ذ ص ض ط ظ</span>, and also <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ع</span>) appear almost only in words borrowed from Arabic, so seeing one is a hint about where the word came from.

Two more letters have merged in the standard Persian of Iran: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">غ</span> and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ق</span> are usually said the same, a sound made far back in the throat that our chart gives as a French r for both. <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">قهوه</span> (ghahve, coffee, #476) and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">غذا</span> (ghazâ, food) start with the same sound. And <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ع</span> is a light catch in the throat, or nothing at all: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">عشق</span> (eshgh, love, #267).

## Joining: why letters change shape

Persian is always written joined up, in print as well as by hand. Most letters connect to the next letter, and a letter's shape changes depending on whether it stands alone, starts a group, sits in the middle or ends it.

| Letter | Alone | Start | Middle | End |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| be | ب | بـ | ـبـ | ـب |
| he (two-eyed) | ه | هـ | ـهـ | ـه |
| dal | د | د | ـد | ـد |

The main pattern is easy: in the middle of a word, most letters shrink to a short shape and the dots do the work. <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ب</span>, <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">پ</span>, <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ت</span> and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ث</span> share one body and differ only in their dots, as do <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ج</span>, <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">چ</span>, <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ح</span> and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">خ</span>. <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ه</span> changes the most, so learn its four shapes on purpose.

### The seven letters that never join forward

Seven letters connect to the letter before them but never to the letter after: **<span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ا د ذ ر ز ژ و</span>**. Arabic has six of them; Persian adds <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ژ</span>. After one of these, the next letter starts fresh, which leaves little gaps inside a word. That is why <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">دوست</span> (dust, friend, #49) looks like three pieces: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">د</span> and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">و</span> each stand alone, then <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ست</span> is joined. <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">کتاب</span> (ketâb, book, #367) breaks after the alef into <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">کتا</span> and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ب</span>.

Persian typing also uses a half-space, an invisible character that stops a join without adding a full space. One common place for it is the verb prefix *mi-*, written as a separate piece before the verb, as in <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">می‌خواهم</span> (mikhâham, I want). Word lists that split text at spaces see <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">می</span> on its own; ours ranks it #26 and glosses it as wine, a real but much rarer meaning.

## Vowels: three written, three left out

Persian has six vowels. The three long ones have letters:

| Vowel | Written with | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| â, the a in father | ا, or آ at the start of a word | آب (âb, water, #194) |
| i, the ee in see | ی | ایران (Irân, Iran) |
| u, the oo in boot | و | روز (ruz, day, #77) |

The three short ones, **a** (as in *cat*), **e** (as in *bet*) and **o** (as in *more*, but short), have optional marks called fathe, kasre and zamme (or zebar, zir and pish), placed above or below the letter. Children's books, dictionaries and learner materials print them; newspapers, signs and chat messages don't. So <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">پدر</span> is written p-d-r, and you read *pedar* because you know the word. <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">دختر</span> (dokhtar, daughter, #128) is d-kh-t-r.

At the start of a word, a short vowel needs a letter to sit on, and that letter is alef: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">اسم</span> (esm, name, #147), <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">امروز</span> (emruz, today, #121).

Three habits of the vowel letters are worth knowing early:

- **<span lang="fa" dir="rtl">و</span>** is *v* at the start of a syllable, *u* inside a word, and in a few very common words a short *o*: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">تو</span> (to, you, #6) and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">دو</span> (do, two, #69). In the combination <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">خوا</span> it is silent: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">خواهر</span> (sister, #546) is *khâhar*.
- **<span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ه</span>** at the end of a word is usually the vowel *e*, not *h*: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">بچه</span> (bache, child, #46), <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">خانه</span> (khâne, house, #597).
- **Written and spoken Persian differ.** Our list, which includes a lot of everyday speech, ranks the spoken form <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">خونه</span> (khune, #56) far above the written <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">خانه</span>, and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">نون</span> (nun, bread, #1,240) above <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">نان</span> (nân, #2,179). In Tehran speech, *ân* often becomes *un*.

## The ezafe, briefly

When a Persian noun is followed by an adjective or an owner, the two are linked by a short *-e*, called the **ezafe**. <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">دوست خوب</span> is *dust-e khub*, a good friend (#49 and #36), and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">کتاب من</span> is *ketâb-e man*, my book (#367 and #3).

The catch for readers is that after a consonant the ezafe is normally not written. You hear *dust-e khub*, but the page shows only two words side by side, and you add the *-e* yourself. After a word that ends in a vowel, it becomes *-ye* and is written with <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ی</span>, as in <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">دانشجوی خوب</span> (dâneshju-ye khub, a good student).

## FAQ

### How many letters are in the Persian alphabet?

Thirty-two: the 28 letters of the Arabic alphabet plus four Persian additions, <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">پ</span> (p), <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">چ</span> (ch), <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ژ</span> (zh) and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">گ</span> (g).

### Is the Persian alphabet the same as the Arabic alphabet?

Almost. Persian uses the Arabic script with four extra letters, writes kaf and ye in slightly different shapes, and says several Arabic letters alike: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ز ذ ض ظ</span> are all z, and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">س ث ص</span> are all s. See our [Arabic alphabet guide](/arabic-alphabet-guide) for the original 28.

### Are vowels written in Persian?

The long vowels â, i and u are written with <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ا</span>, <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ی</span> and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">و</span>. The short vowels a, e and o have optional marks that everyday text leaves out, so you learn them with each word.

### Which Persian letters don't connect?

Seven: <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ا</span>, <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">د</span>, <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ذ</span>, <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ر</span>, <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ز</span>, <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ژ</span> and <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">و</span>. They join the letter before them but never the letter after, which is why some Persian words look broken into pieces.

### What is the ezafe in Persian?

A short -e that links a noun to an adjective or an owner, as in dust-e khub (good friend). After a consonant it is usually not written; after a vowel it becomes -ye and is written with <span lang="fa" dir="rtl">ی</span>.
