## How to pick an app to practice German

**Know which German you will hear.** Standard German (Hochdeutsch) is understood everywhere, but the German-speaking world has three main flavors:

- **Germany** has strong regional accents, but most people switch to something close to the standard with learners.
- **Austria** has its own vocabulary: *Jänner* for January, *Paradeiser* for tomato, and *Grüß Gott* as an everyday greeting (also common in Bavaria).
- **Switzerland** is different again. People speak Swiss German dialects (Schweizerdeutsch) in daily life, which can be hard even for Germans to follow, but they write Swiss Standard German, which is very close to Germany's and never uses ß.

If you are moving to Vienna or Zurich, filter partners by country early. Otherwise, a partner from anywhere will teach you German you can use everywhere.

**Look for correction tools.** German mistakes are small and frequent: *der* instead of *den*, a verb in the wrong place, a missing ending on an adjective. An app where partners can mark up your exact sentence beats one where they have to retype it.

**Match the format to your level.** Writing gives you time to work out cases and word order, so text chat suits beginners. Once you can build sentences on paper, live calls force you to do it at speaking speed.

**Be ready to give back.** Many German speakers already speak good English and want to polish it, so English speakers usually find partners. Some will switch to English to be helpful; agree on how you split the time.

## Language exchange apps for German

### Tandem

[Tandem](https://tandem.net) launched in 2015 and has its main offices in Berlin. It reviews new members and focuses on one-to-one conversations with text, voice messages and calls, which suits German well: one patient partner who knows your recurring mistakes is worth more than twenty short chats.

- **Best for:** a regular weekly call, and filtering for a partner in a specific country.
- **Keep in mind:** approval can take time, and some filters and unlimited translation are in Tandem Pro.

### HelloTalk

[HelloTalk](https://www.hellotalk.com) is the biggest exchange community, so German speakers are easy to find. Its **Moments** feed lets you post a few sentences and get corrections from several native speakers, handy for seeing whether a word order sounds natural or only correct.

- **Best for:** volume, and live group audio in Voicerooms.

## German-specific resources worth adding

**[DW Learn German](https://learngerman.dw.com)** from Deutsche Welle, Germany's international broadcaster, is free on the web and as an app. Its best-known course, **Nicos Weg**, follows a young man arriving in Germany through short video episodes with exercises, from A1 to B1. It gives you the structure an exchange lacks.

**Easy German**, part of the Easy Languages network on YouTube, films street interviews in Germany with subtitles in German and English. It shows you how people actually talk, including the fillers (*also*, *halt*, *eben*) that textbooks leave out.

## Du or Sie in a German language exchange

- **Sie is the polite default** with strangers, older people and anyone in a professional setting. It takes plural verb forms and is always capitalized.
- **Du is normal between exchange partners,** especially online and among people of similar age. Most partners on an app will use du from the start.
- **If in doubt, ask.** *Wollen wir uns duzen?* ("Shall we say du to each other?") is the standard question.
- **Ihr is the plural of du.** Talking to two friends at once, you use ihr.

## Your first message in German

Keep it short, friendly and specific to their profile.

> Hallo Lena! Ich heiße Sam und komme aus Kanada. Ich lerne Deutsch und habe gesehen, dass du Englisch lernst. Wollen wir uns gegenseitig helfen? Ich helfe dir mit Englisch, und du korrigierst mein Deutsch.

In English: "Hi Lena! My name is Sam and I'm from Canada. I'm learning German and saw that you're learning English. Shall we help each other? I help you with English, and you correct my German." Notice *lernst* at the end of the *dass* clause: German sends the verb to the end of a subordinate clause, and your partner will notice if you get it right.

## Tips for German learners on exchange apps

**Learn every noun with its article.** *Der Tisch*, *die Lampe*, *das Buch*. German capitalizes all nouns, so they are easy to spot in your partner's messages; copy the article along with the word.

**Ask for corrections by name.** Tell your partner you want help with cases (especially accusative and dative after prepositions), adjective endings and verb position. Specific requests get specific corrections.

**Set up a German keyboard.** Typing ä, ö, ü and ß takes seconds. If you cannot, write ae, oe, ue and ss; every German speaker understands it.

**Do not fear compound words.** *Handschuh* is "hand shoe", a glove. Ask your partner to split long words for you and you will start guessing new ones correctly.

**Send voice messages.** German spelling is fairly regular, but sounds like *ü*, *ch* and the final *-er* need an ear. A short voice message a day, and one back from your partner, adds up quickly.

## FAQ

### What is the best app to practice German with native speakers?

Tandem is a strong pick for a steady one-to-one partner, and HelloTalk has the largest community. LangX suits learners who want unlimited corrections and no ads. For a paid teacher, italki and Preply both have many German tutors.

### Can I practice German speaking for free?

Yes. Tandem and HelloTalk include free calls with partners, and Conversation Exchange helps you meet someone in person. LangX supports voice messages but not live calls. DW Learn German is a free course, though not a way to talk to people.

### Should I use du or Sie with a language exchange partner?

Du is normal between exchange partners. Use Sie with someone much older or in a formal setting, and if you are unsure, ask "Wollen wir uns duzen?"

### Is Swiss German the same as German?

Swiss people write Standard German, which you can learn anywhere, but they speak Swiss German dialects in daily life. If you are moving to German-speaking Switzerland, find a Swiss partner early to get used to hearing it.
