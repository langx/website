The best apps to practice Turkish with native speakers are **HelloTalk** and **Tandem**, the two largest exchange communities, where Turkish speakers learning English or German are easy to find. **LangX** suits learners who want every message open to correction and no ads, and **italki** or **Preply** give you a paid tutor on video. Turkish is built differently from English. Words grow by adding suffixes one after another, the vowels in those suffixes change to match the word, and the verb comes at the end of the sentence. None of it is hard once it clicks, but it clicks faster when a native speaker corrects *evlerde* into *evlerimizde* in your own sentence than when you read the rule.

## How to pick an app to practice Turkish

**Know what makes Turkish different.** Three features shape every conversation:

- **Vowel harmony.** Suffixes change their vowels to match the last vowel of the word. The plural is *-ler* in *evler* (houses) but *-lar* in *kitaplar* (books). The question particle can be *mi*, *mı*, *mu* or *mü*: *güzel mi?* (is it nice?), *doğru mu?* (is it right?).
- **Agglutination.** Meaning is stacked onto one word. *Ev* is house, *evler* houses, *evlerimiz* our houses, *evlerimizde* in our houses, *evlerimizden* from our houses. Where English needs three words, Turkish uses one.
- **Verb last.** *Ben Türkçe öğreniyorum* is literally "I Turkish am-learning".

These are exactly the mistakes a partner can fix line by line, so correction tools matter.

**Turkish speakers are not only in Turkey.** Turkish is also spoken in Cyprus and by large communities in Germany, the Netherlands, Austria and elsewhere in Europe. If you live in one of those countries, a partner for a face-to-face exchange may be closer than you think.

**Match the format to your level.** Text chat gives you time to build words suffix by suffix. Turkish spelling matches the sound almost perfectly, so voice messages are an easy next step.

**Be ready to give back.** Many Turkish speakers are learning English, and many are learning German. Either is a strong offer in an exchange.

## Language exchange apps for Turkish

### HelloTalk

[HelloTalk](https://www.hellotalk.com) is the biggest exchange community, and Turkish speakers are active on it. Its **Moments** feed lets you post a few sentences and get corrections from several native speakers, handy for checking whether a long word you built is actually one a Turk would say.

- **Best for:** volume, and live group audio in Voicerooms.

[Tandem](https://tandem.net) reviews new members and focuses on one-to-one conversations with text, voice messages and calls. For Turkish, a single patient partner who knows your recurring suffix mistakes is worth more than many short chats.

## Turkish-specific resources worth adding

**[The Yunus Emre Institute's portal](https://turkce.yee.org.tr/en/)** is a free Turkish course from the institute that promotes Turkish language and culture abroad. Its video lessons, prepared by Turkish teachers, are organized by CEFR level, with reading, listening, grammar and vocabulary activities. It is one of the most complete free Turkish courses you can find.

**Easy Turkish**, part of the Easy Languages network on YouTube, films street interviews with subtitles in Turkish and English, so you hear how people really talk.

**Frequency lists and tests.** Start with our [most common Turkish words](/tools/most-common-words/turkish), and you will see vowel harmony in the ranking itself: *mi* is number 6 and *mı* is number 15, *de* is number 7 and *da* is number 14. They are the same words, spelled to match the words around them. Then check your level with the [Turkish vocabulary test](/tools/vocabulary-test/turkish).

**The alphabet.** Turkish has used the Latin alphabet since 1928, with a few extra letters: *ç*, *ğ*, *ı*, *ö*, *ş* and *ü*. The one that catches everyone is the dotless *ı*, which is a separate letter from *i*: *kır* is countryside, *kir* is dirt. The capital of *i* is *İ*, with a dot.

## Sen or siz in a Turkish language exchange

- **Siz is the polite "you"** for strangers, older people, customers and anyone in a professional setting. It is also the plural "you".
- **Sen is normal between exchange partners** of similar age, and most young Turks switch to it quickly online.
- **Start with siz if you are unsure.** *Sana sen diyebilir miyim?* ("Can I call you sen?") is a polite way to ask.
- **Learn the terms of address.** An older man is often *abi* (big brother) and an older woman *abla* (big sister), even strangers; *hocam* ("my teacher") is a friendly, respectful way to address someone helping you.

## Your first message in Turkish

Build your first message only from phrases you are certain of. Short, correct sentences make a better impression than an ambitious one with four wrong suffixes.

> Merhaba, nasılsın? Benim adım Sam. Kanadalıyım. Türkçe öğreniyorum. Sen İngilizce öğreniyorsun, değil mi? Birlikte pratik yapalım mı? İngilizcene yardım edebilirim. Hatalarımı düzeltir misin?

In English: "Hello, how are you? My name is Sam. I'm Canadian. I'm learning Turkish. You're learning English, right? Shall we practice together? I can help with your English. Would you correct my mistakes?" Every sentence is short enough to be sure of, and the harmony is at work throughout: *Kanadalı-yım* takes *ı* to match *a*, and *düzeltir misin* takes *i* to match *e*. When your partner replies, ask how they would say the same thing more naturally, and you have your first correction.

Three more phrases you will use every week:

- **... Türkçe nasıl denir?** How do you say... in Turkish?
- **Kulağa doğal geliyor mu?** Does it sound natural?
- **Daha yavaş konuşabilir misin?** Can you speak more slowly?

## Tips for Turkish learners on exchange apps

**Ask for corrections by name.** Ask for help with vowel harmony, the case endings (*-i*, *-e*, *-de*, *-den* and their variants) and word order. Specific requests get specific corrections.

**Ask your partner to split long words.** When you meet a word like *görüşemedik*, ask for it in pieces: *görüş-e-me-di-k*, "we could not meet". Seeing the parts once teaches you to read the next long word yourself.

**Set up a Turkish keyboard.** You need *ı*, *ğ*, *ş*, *ç*, *ö* and *ü*. Many Turks skip them in casual chat (*nasilsin* for *nasılsın*), but write the proper letters yourself: *ı* and *i* are different sounds.

**Use voice messages for ğ and ı.** *Ğ* is not pronounced like a g; it lengthens the vowel before it, as in *dağ* (mountain). *I* is a sound English does not have. A short voice message a day, and one back from your partner, fixes both.

**Learn the fixed expressions.** *Kolay gelsin* (said to someone working), *geçmiş olsun* (get well soon, or sorry for your trouble) and *eline sağlık* (said after someone cooks for you) come up constantly and have no neat English equivalent. Using one in the right place makes a partner smile.

## FAQ

### What is the best app to practice Turkish with native speakers?

HelloTalk has the largest community and Tandem is strong for a steady one-to-one partner. LangX suits learners who want unlimited corrections and no ads. For a paid teacher, italki and Preply both have Turkish tutors.

### Can I practice Turkish speaking for free?

Yes. HelloTalk and Tandem include free calls with partners, and Conversation Exchange helps you meet someone in person. LangX supports voice messages but not live calls.

### What is vowel harmony in Turkish?

It is the rule that suffixes change their vowels to match the last vowel of the word. That is why the plural is *evler* but *kitaplar*, and why the question particle can be *mi*, *mı*, *mu* or *mü*.

### Should I use sen or siz with a language exchange partner?

Sen is normal between exchange partners of similar age. Use siz with someone older or in a formal setting, and if you are unsure, ask "Sana sen diyebilir miyim?"

### Is there an AI app for Turkish speaking practice?

At the time of writing, Speak and Praktika do not teach Turkish, so a human partner or a tutor is the more reliable way to practice speaking.
