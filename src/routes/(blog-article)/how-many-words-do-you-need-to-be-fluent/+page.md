For everyday conversation, most learners need somewhere around **2,000 to 3,000
word families**; to follow speech comfortably, research points to about **6,000
to 7,000**; and to read unassisted, about **8,000 to 9,000** (Nation, 2006).
Those numbers are about understanding. For speaking, you can get a long way with
far fewer words, as long as they are the most frequent ones and you have used
them in real conversations.

The rest of this post explains where those figures come from, what a "word"
actually means in them, and how to turn them into a plan.

## What counts as a "word"?

Before any number makes sense, you need to know what is being counted. There are
three common units:

- **Word form:** every spelling counts separately. *Talk*, *talks*, *talked*
  and *talking* are four words.
- **Lemma:** a base word and its inflections. The four forms above are one
  lemma.
- **Word family:** a base word plus its inflections *and* closely related
  derived words. *Talk*, *talker* and *talkative* can all belong to one family.

Most research on how many words you need counts **word families**, which is the
most generous unit. A figure like "8,000 word families" represents many more
individual word forms. Keep that in mind when you compare it with a word list:
the frequency lists on our [most common words](/tools/most-common-words) pages,
for example, count word forms as they appear in real speech, so a Spanish list
contains *pensé* ("I thought") and *tengas* ("you have," subjunctive) as their
own entries.

## What the research says

The key idea in vocabulary research is **lexical coverage**: the percentage of
words in a text or conversation that you know. Researchers test how comprehension
changes as coverage rises, then work out how big a vocabulary gives you that
coverage.

### 95% vs 98% coverage

Two thresholds come up again and again:

- **98% coverage** is often treated as the level for comfortable, unassisted
  understanding. That means one unknown word in every fifty.
- **95% coverage** gives reasonable but less complete understanding, one
  unknown word in every twenty, which in a fast conversation is a lot.

Laufer and Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) described these as an "optimal" and a
"minimal" threshold for reading: about **8,000 word families** for 98% coverage
and about **4,000 to 5,000** for 95%.

### Reading needs more words than listening

Paul Nation's widely cited study, "How large a vocabulary is needed for reading
and listening?" (Nation, 2006), estimated that if 98% coverage is the target,
you need:

- **8,000 to 9,000 word families** to read written text, such as novels and
  newspapers
- **6,000 to 7,000 word families** to understand spoken text

Speech needs fewer words because conversation reuses a smaller, more frequent
vocabulary than writing does.

For listening, van Zeeland and Schmitt (2013) found that learners could still
understand spoken passages reasonably well at 95% coverage, and estimated that
reaching 95% coverage of everyday spoken English takes roughly **2,000 to 3,000
word families**. That is the figure behind the "2,000 to 3,000 words for
conversation" rule of thumb.

| Goal | Coverage | Approximate vocabulary | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Follow everyday speech reasonably well | 95% | 2,000 to 3,000 word families | van Zeeland and Schmitt (2013) |
| Read with minimal comprehension | 95% | 4,000 to 5,000 word families | Laufer and Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) |
| Understand speech comfortably | 98% | 6,000 to 7,000 word families | Nation (2006) |
| Read unassisted | 98% | 8,000 to 9,000 word families | Nation (2006) |

<Callout type="warning">
These studies were done on English. Other languages package meaning differently:
a language with lots of inflection, like Russian or Turkish, has many more word
forms per word family, and Chinese builds most words out of a few thousand
characters. The ranges are a useful guide, not a precise target for every
language.
</Callout>

## How many words for each CEFR level?

The Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR) describes levels from A1 to C2
by what you can do, not by how many words you know, so there is no official word
count per level. Researchers have tried to estimate one. Milton and Alexiou
(2009) tested learners of English, French and Greek as foreign languages and
matched their vocabulary size to their CEFR level. For English as a foreign
language, their estimates were roughly:

| CEFR level | Estimated vocabulary (English as a foreign language) |
|---|---|
| A1 | fewer than 1,500 words |
| A2 | 1,500 to 2,500 |
| B1 | 2,750 to 3,250 |
| B2 | 3,250 to 3,750 |
| C1 | 3,750 to 4,500 |
| C2 | 4,500 to 5,000 |

Treat these as estimates from one line of research, not a standard. The test
used only measured knowledge of the most frequent 5,000 words, so the top of
the scale is capped, and the figures for French learners in the same study were
noticeably lower at each level. The takeaway is the shape: each level adds a
thousand or so of the next most frequent words.

## So what does "fluent" mean in words?

Fluency is not a single number, but here is a practical way to think about it:

- **Around 500 to 1,000 of the most frequent words:** survival conversations.
  You can introduce yourself, ask questions, and get through simple exchanges
  with a patient partner.
- **Around 2,000 to 3,000:** conversational. You can follow most everyday talk
  on familiar topics and fill the gaps from context.
- **Around 5,000 and up:** comfortable. Films, podcasts and group conversations
  stop being exhausting.
- **8,000 and beyond:** reading novels and newspapers without a dictionary.

The first two stages are where most learners want to be, and they are much
closer than "fluency" makes them sound, provided you learn the right words.

## A practical plan

1. **Take the vocabulary test** for your language and note roughly where your
   knowledge drops off.
2. **Learn the next 500 to 1,000 words in frequency order** with spaced
   repetition. Ten to twenty new words a day is sustainable for most people;
   at 15 a day, 1,000 words takes a little over two months.
3. **Learn words in phrases, not alone.** "Can I have" teaches more than *have*.
   The example sentences on the word list pages help here.
4. **Use every new word in a real sentence within a few days**, in writing or
   out loud.
5. **Retest every month or two.** Watching the estimate climb is a good
   antidote to feeling stuck.
6. **Past about 3,000 words, shift toward input.** Reading, podcasts and
   shows will teach you the long tail faster than flashcards.

## A word list is half the job

Knowing that *cansado* means "tired" is recognition. Saying "estoy muy cansado
hoy" to someone who replies is production, and the two are stored differently.
A word you have only seen on a flashcard is fragile; a word you have used in a
conversation, and been corrected on, tends to stay.

## FAQ

### Is 1,000 words enough to have a conversation?

For simple, everyday conversations with a patient partner, often yes, if they
are the 1,000 most frequent words. You will miss a lot of what fast native
speakers say, which is why research on comfortable listening points to several
thousand word families.

### How many words do you need to be fluent in Spanish?

The research above was done on English, but it is a reasonable guide for
Spanish: roughly 2,000 to 3,000 word families to follow everyday conversation,
and more for films and books. Start with the
[most common Spanish words](/tools/most-common-words/spanish) and measure your
progress with the [Spanish vocabulary test](/tools/vocabulary-test/spanish).

### How many words do I know in a language?

Take a [vocabulary test](/tools/vocabulary-test). Ours samples words from every
frequency band and gives a rough estimate in two minutes. It is an estimate, not
a certificate, but it is good for tracking progress.

### How long does it take to learn 3,000 words?

At 10 new words a day with regular review, about ten months; at 20 a day, about
five. Real life adds forgetting and missed days, so plan for longer, and use the
words in conversation so fewer of them slip away.

### Is it better to learn words from a list or from context?

Both. A frequency list is the most efficient way to build the first few thousand
words; context from reading, listening and conversation is how those words
become usable and how you pick up the rarer ones later.
