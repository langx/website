To do a language exchange well, split the time evenly between both languages, come with a few topics, agree on how you want to be corrected, and write down what you learn. Then keep showing up: a short exchange every day or two teaches far more than a long one every month.

Those are the essentials. The twelve rules below are what separates exchanges that fizzle after a week from ones that last for a year and actually move your level. They apply to text, voice and video, and to in-person meetings. If you still need a partner, start with [how to find a language exchange partner](/how-to-find-a-language-exchange-partner).

## Rule 1: Split the time 50/50, and protect it

Reciprocity is the foundation of every language exchange: both people should give and get the same. In practice that means equal time in each language.

- **Live calls:** use a timer. Twenty minutes in one language, twenty in the other, is a good start.
- **Text:** alternate by message, by day, or reply to each message in the language it was written in.
- **In person:** switch at a natural break, like a coffee refill.

The split drifts toward whichever language is stronger between you. If your partner's English is much better than your Spanish, the chat will slide into English unless someone steers it back. Be that person, kindly: "Can we switch back to Spanish for a bit?"

## Rule 2: Agree on the basics in the first conversation

Two minutes of setup saves weeks of awkwardness. Agree on:

- Which language when (see rule 1)
- How often you will talk, and roughly how long
- How much correction each of you wants (see rule 4)
- Whether you prefer text, voice notes or calls

## Rule 3: Prepare two or three topics

"So... what do you want to talk about?" is where exchanges go to die. Before each session, pick two or three topics and look up a few words you will need for them. You will speak more, and more comfortably.

If you need ideas, we put together [100 language exchange conversation topics and questions, organized by level](/language-exchange-conversation-topics).

## Rule 4: Tell your partner how to correct you

Correction is the most valuable thing a partner can give you, and the most awkward to get right. Some learners want every mistake fixed; others lose their nerve if every sentence comes back covered in red. Neither is wrong, but your partner cannot guess. Say it:

- "Please correct every mistake in my messages, even small ones."
- "Only correct mistakes that change the meaning or sound really unnatural."
- "During calls, let me finish, then tell me the two or three biggest things."

Ask for the natural version, not only the correct one. A sentence can be grammatically fine and still sound like a textbook. "How would you say this?" gets you the phrasing native speakers actually use.

## Rule 5: Give corrections the way you would want them

When you are the expert, a few habits make your corrections useful rather than discouraging:

- **Show the fix, do not just flag the error.** Rewrite the sentence the way you would say it.
- **Explain briefly if you can**, and say so if you cannot. "It just sounds better" is honest and still helpful.
- **Pick your battles.** For a beginner, fix what blocks meaning first.
- **Correct in writing when possible.** A written correction can be reviewed later; a spoken one is often forgotten by the end of the call.

## Rule 6: Record every new word and correction

If you do not write it down, you will not remember it. Keep one running list per partner or per language: a notebook, a notes app, or a flashcard deck. For each item, note:

- The word or phrase
- The sentence it came up in
- The correction, if you got something wrong

Review the list before your next session and try to use two or three items from it. That is what turns a pleasant chat into actual learning. For more on making words stick, see [how to remember your foreign languages even after years](/stop-the-struggle-how-to-remember-your-foreign-languages-even-after-years).

## Rule 7: Use translation sparingly

Translation is a crutch, and crutches are fine when you need one. The problem is leaning on it when you do not.

- **Do** translate a word you genuinely cannot guess, or a message you have read twice and still do not understand.
- **Do not** write your whole message in your own language and paste the translation. You learn nothing, and your partner corrects the translator, not you.
- **Try first, then check.** Write your best attempt, then translate only the parts you were unsure about.

## Rule 8: Use voice notes, not just text

Text is great for accuracy, but it never trains your mouth or your ear. Voice notes are the easy middle ground between text chat and a scheduled call:

- No time zone juggling. Record when you are free, listen when they are.
- You can re-record until you are happy, which takes the pressure off.
- Your partner can hear and correct your pronunciation, and you get to hear theirs as many times as you like.

A simple routine: once a day, send a 30-second voice note about something that happened, and ask your partner to reply the same way. ## Rule 9: Talk about real things

The best exchanges stop feeling like practice. Talk about your actual week, your job, the show you are watching, the dinner you burned. You remember language that is attached to something you care about, and your partner learns about your life and culture along the way.

Photos help a lot here. A picture of your street, your market or your lunch starts a conversation that a list of questions never will.

## Rule 10: Keep it consistent

A little, often, beats a lot, rarely. Ten minutes of messages every day keeps the language active in your head and keeps the exchange alive. A two-hour call once a month does neither.

## Rule 11: Have more than one partner

Partners get busy, travel, lose interest or simply run out of things to say. With two or three exchanges going, one quiet partner does not stop your practice. Different people also expose you to different accents, vocabulary and ways of speaking, which is exactly what you need to understand real conversations.

## Rule 12: End gracefully when it is time

Not every exchange lasts forever, and that is fine. If you need to stop, say so:

> "I've really enjoyed our chats, but I don't have much time for the next few months. Thank you for all the help with my German, and good luck with your English!"

It takes thirty seconds, it is kinder than disappearing, and it keeps the door open if you want to pick it up again later.

## A simple session structure you can copy

For a 40-minute call:

| Minutes | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| 0–3 | Small talk, agree which language goes first |
| 3–20 | Language A: your prepared topic, partner corrects |
| 20–37 | Language B: their prepared topic, you correct |
| 37–40 | Quick recap: each person names two things they learned |

For text, the rhythm is looser: a message or two a day in each language, corrections on the messages that need them, and a question at the end of every reply so the conversation keeps moving.

## FAQ

### How long should a language exchange session be?

Thirty to sixty minutes is typical for live calls, split evenly between the two languages. For text exchanges, a few messages a day is enough to make steady progress.

### Should I correct every mistake my partner makes?

Only if they asked you to. Agree on a correction style first, then focus on mistakes that affect meaning or sound very unnatural, and show the corrected sentence rather than just pointing out the error.

### What do you talk about in a language exchange?

Your daily life, work or studies, food, travel, culture, plans and opinions. Prepare two or three topics beforehand; our list of [language exchange conversation topics](/language-exchange-conversation-topics) has 100 questions by level.

### How do I stop the conversation from switching to English?

Agree on a time split up front, use a timer on calls, and gently steer back when it drifts. If the level gap is large, try text or voice notes in your target language, where you have time to think.
