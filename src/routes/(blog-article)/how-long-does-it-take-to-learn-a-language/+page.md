For an English speaker, reaching professional working proficiency takes about
**550 to 700 class hours for languages close to English** (Spanish, French,
Italian, Dutch, Portuguese, the Scandinavian languages) and about **2,200 class
hours for Arabic, Chinese, Japanese and Korean**, according to the US Foreign
Service Institute. That is full-time study. At a few hours a week, the same goal
takes years, while a solid conversational level (CEFR B1) is realistic in about
one to two years for most people.

The honest answer depends on three things: which language, what you mean by
"learn," and how many hours a week you actually put in. This guide gives you the
published numbers, shows how to turn them into a timeline for your own week, and
covers what genuinely shortens it.

## What the Foreign Service Institute says

The Foreign Service Institute (FSI) trains US diplomats, and it publishes how
long its students usually take to reach a score of 3 in speaking and listening
on the Interagency Language Roundtable (ILR) scale, often called "general
professional proficiency." These are the figures on the [State Department's
foreign language training
page](https://www.state.gov/national-foreign-affairs-training-center/foreign-language-training),
checked in September 2026:

| FSI category | Weeks of training | Class hours | Languages |
|---|---|---|---|
| Category I: languages close to English | 24 to 30 | 552 to 690 | Danish, Dutch, Italian, Norwegian, Portuguese, Romanian, Swedish (24 weeks); French, Spanish (30 weeks) |
| Category II | About 36 | 828 | German, Haitian Creole, Indonesian, Malay, Swahili |
| Category III: "hard languages" | About 44 | 1,012 | Many, including Russian, Greek, Hindi, Hebrew, Polish, Turkish, Thai, Vietnamese, Ukrainian |
| Category IV: "super-hard languages" | 88 | 2,200 | Arabic, Chinese (Cantonese and Mandarin), Japanese, Korean |

Two details on that page matter more than the table itself. First, a typical FSI
week is **23 hours in class plus 17 hours of self-study**, so the class-hour
figure is only part of the effort. Second, the FSI says the actual time varies
with the learner's natural ability, prior language-learning experience and time
spent in the classroom.

<Callout type="info">
You will often see older FSI figures quoted online, such as 600 to 750 hours for
Category I, 900 for Category II and 1,100 for Category III. Those come from an
earlier version of the same page. The current page lists the lower class-hour
numbers above, and it states the 23 + 17 hour weekly split explicitly.
</Callout>

## What does "learn a language" actually mean?

"How long does it take to learn a language" has no single answer because
"learn" means very different things. The most common yardstick is the Common
European Framework of Reference (CEFR), which describes six levels by what you
can do:

- **A1 and A2 (basic):** introduce yourself, handle simple, familiar exchanges,
  shop, order, ask directions.
- **B1 (threshold):** get by in most everyday situations, talk about your life,
  plans and opinions, even if slowly and with mistakes.
- **B2 (vantage):** hold a spontaneous conversation with native speakers without
  much strain on either side. Most people mean something like this when they say
  "fluent."
- **C1 and C2 (proficient):** use the language flexibly for work and study,
  follow nuance, humor and fast speech.

The FSI's goal, ILR level 3, is a professional standard. It is often compared to
around C1, although the two scales were built for different purposes and do not
map exactly. In other words, the FSI numbers describe a high bar, well beyond
chatting comfortably with a friend.

### Hours per CEFR level

The CEFR itself does not assign hours to levels. The most widely cited estimate
comes from **Cambridge English**, which suggests approximate guided learning
hours for learners of English to reach each level, counted from the start:

| CEFR level | Approximate cumulative guided learning hours (Cambridge English, for English) |
|---|---|
| A2 | 180 to 200 |
| B1 | 350 to 400 |
| B2 | 500 to 600 |
| C1 | 700 to 800 |
| C2 | 1,000 to 1,200 |

Cambridge presents these as a guideline only. "Guided learning hours" means
time in a class or program, including homework, and the figures are for English.
For an English speaker learning a Category IV language, expect every level to
take noticeably longer.

## How weekly hours turn into months

The published figures are in hours; your life is in weeks. Here is how the two
connect. **The numbers below are an illustration built from simple arithmetic,
not a prediction or a study.** They assume the hours are real, focused practice
and that you keep going without long breaks.

**Example 1: reaching B1, using Cambridge's 350 to 400 hours as a rough guide.**

| Your weekly time | Weeks needed (350 to 400 hours) | Roughly |
|---|---|---|
| 3 hours a week | 117 to 133 weeks | 2.2 to 2.6 years |
| 5 hours a week | 70 to 80 weeks | 16 to 18 months |
| 7 hours a week (1 hour a day) | 50 to 57 weeks | about a year |
| 10 hours a week | 35 to 40 weeks | 8 to 9 months |

The arithmetic: 350 hours divided by 5 hours a week is 70 weeks, and 400 divided
by 5 is 80. Divide by about 4.3 to get months.

**Example 2: the FSI goal for Spanish.** Spanish is listed at 30 weeks. At 23
class hours plus 17 self-study hours, that is 30 × 40 = **about 1,200 hours of
total effort**. At 10 hours a week, 1,200 hours is 120 weeks, or roughly two and
a third years. At 5 hours a week, it is closer to five years.

That second example is sobering, but remember what it measures: a professional
level, reached by adults studying under pressure for a diplomatic posting. Most
learners are well served by B1 or B2, which the same arithmetic puts within
reach in roughly one to two and a half years of steady practice.

<Callout type="warning">
Hours are not all equal. An hour of passive listening while you cook is not the
same as an hour of conversation where someone corrects you. Both count, but if
you only do the easy kind, expect the slow end of every range above.
</Callout>

## Why some people learn faster than others

The FSI names ability, experience and time in class. In practice, these are the
factors that move your timeline the most:

1. **How close the language is to one you know.** English speakers get a head
   start in Romance and Germanic languages from shared vocabulary. A Spanish
   speaker learning Italian or Portuguese starts even further ahead; our
   [Italian and Spanish comparison](/tools/similar/italian-and-spanish) shows
   how much overlaps.
2. **A second foreign language is faster than the first.** Knowing how to
   learn a language, how to handle grammar, drill vocabulary and survive
   confusion, carries over.
3. **A new writing system.** Every Category I and II language uses the Latin
   alphabet. A new script is a real, separate task; the
   [alphabet tools](/tools/alphabet/korean) help with the first step.
4. **Consistency.** Thirty minutes every day beats a four-hour burst once a
   week, because you keep reviewing before you forget. We made the case in
   [why consistent practice is key](/why-consistent-practice-is-key-to-language-learning-success).
5. **How much you speak.** Learners who only read and listen often understand
   far more than they can say. Speaking is a separate skill that needs its own
   hours.

### Learn the most frequent words first

A small set of words makes up most of everyday speech, which is why frequency
lists are such an efficient start. Our guide to [how many words you need to be
fluent](/how-many-words-do-you-need-to-be-fluent) covers the vocabulary research,
and the [most common words lists](/tools/most-common-words) give you the first
thousand words in dozens of languages, for example
[Spanish](/tools/most-common-words/spanish).

### Use spaced repetition for vocabulary

Reviewing a word just before you would forget it is the most time-efficient way
to keep it. Ten to twenty minutes a day with a flashcard app is enough for most
learners.

### Start speaking early

Waiting until you "know enough" to speak is the most common way to lose a year.
Start with short, low-pressure exchanges, voice messages or text chat, and build
up. Our guide on [how to improve your speaking in a foreign
language](/how-to-improve-speaking-skills-in-a-foreign-language) has a 30-day
plan.

### Get corrected

Mistakes that nobody points out tend to stick. A tutor, a teacher or a
[language exchange partner](/how-to-find-a-language-exchange-partner) who
corrects you speeds things up because you stop practicing errors.

This is where an app like [LangX](/) fits. It matches you with people who speak
the language you are learning and are learning yours, so the exchange works in
both directions. You can hold any message to correct it, and corrections are
unlimited on every plan, including Free. You can send voice and photo messages,
and translation is built into the chat. LangX does not do live calls, so if you
want real-time conversation you will need to add a tutor or a meetup.

### Measure progress so you notice it

Progress feels slow from the inside. Take a vocabulary test every couple of
months, for example the [Spanish vocabulary test](/tools/vocabulary-test/spanish),
and keep old voice recordings so you can hear the difference.

### Make it easy to keep going

Most people who "fail" at a language simply stopped. Pick a daily minimum so
small you will do it on your worst day, and see our tips on [staying
motivated](/how-to-stay-motivated-while-learning-a-new-language).

## Realistic timelines at a glance

Putting the published numbers and the arithmetic together, here is a
reasonable expectation for an English-speaking adult learner doing about **5 to
7 hours of focused practice a week**. Treat it as a planning range, not a
promise.

| Goal | Category I (e.g. Spanish, French) | Category IV (e.g. Japanese, Korean) |
|---|---|---|
| Survival basics (A1 to A2) | 3 to 6 months | 6 to 12 months |
| Everyday conversation (B1) | 1 to 1.5 years | 2 to 3 years |
| Comfortable fluency (B2) | 1.5 to 2.5 years | 3 years or more |
| Professional level (about C1 / ILR 3) | several years at this pace | many years at this pace |

These ranges are extrapolated from the Cambridge and FSI hour figures above,
scaled roughly for the harder languages. Your own pace could be faster or
slower.

## FAQ

### How long does it take to learn Spanish?

The FSI lists Spanish at 30 weeks of full-time training (690 class hours, plus
self-study) to reach professional proficiency. For a conversational B1 level at
around an hour a day, a year is a realistic target.

### Can you learn a language in 3 months?

You can reach a useful basic level in three months, especially in a language
close to English and with daily practice. Fluency in three months is not
realistic for most people; the published hour figures simply do not fit into
that window at a normal weekly pace.

### How many hours a day should I study a language?

Consistency matters more than volume. Thirty to sixty minutes a day, mixing
vocabulary, listening and some speaking or writing, is sustainable for most
people and adds up to 3.5 to 7 hours a week.

### Which languages take longest for English speakers?

Arabic, Mandarin, Cantonese, Japanese and Korean. The FSI puts them in
Category IV at 88 weeks, about three times as long as Spanish or Dutch.

### Does a language exchange make you learn faster?

It adds the part most self-study lacks: real conversation with someone who
corrects you. It works best alongside vocabulary and listening work, not
instead of it. Our guide on [how to do a language
exchange](/how-to-do-a-language-exchange) explains how to set one up well.
