The shadowing technique means listening to a native speaker and speaking along
with them almost at the same moment, a fraction of a second behind, copying
their sounds, rhythm and melody. Done for ten to fifteen minutes a day with short
audio you already understand, it is one of the most direct ways to make your
speech sound more natural and to get your mouth used to saying whole sentences
at normal speed.

This guide goes deeper than the quick version in our post on [how to improve
your speaking skills](/how-to-improve-speaking-skills-in-a-foreign-language): a
full routine, which audio to pick, the mistakes that waste the time, and how to
turn a conversation partner into your best shadowing source.

## What is the shadowing technique?

Shadowing comes from the training of simultaneous interpreters, who practise
repeating a speaker's words in real time before they learn to translate them in
real time. The polyglot Alexander Arguelles made it popular with self-learners,
often shadowing while walking outdoors to keep his energy and volume up.

In language teaching, the best-known academic treatment is Shuhei Kadota's book
*Shadowing as a Practice in Second Language Acquisition* (Routledge, 2019).
Kadota describes shadowing as helping in several ways at once: it sharpens
listening, it gives your short-term memory practice holding and rehearsing new
words and phrases, it rehearses parts of the process of producing speech, and it
trains you to monitor your own output.

The key difference from ordinary "listen and repeat" is timing. With listen and
repeat, you wait for the sentence to end and then say it. With shadowing, you
start speaking while the speaker is still talking. That leaves no time to
translate or think about grammar, so you copy the sound as a whole: where the
stress falls, where the voice rises and drops, which words get swallowed.

## Why shadowing works, and what it won't do

Most learners who have studied for a while can read far better than they can
speak. Part of the gap is physical. Your mouth has never produced these sound
sequences at speed, and your ear has not learned which parts of a sentence
matter. Shadowing attacks both at once, with hundreds of repetitions of real,
natural sentences in a single session.

It is also low-pressure. There is no one waiting for your answer and no one to
judge you, which is why it suits people who find talking to strangers draining
(our [introvert's guide](/mastering-a-new-language-an-introverts-guide) has more
on that).

That last row of limits matters. Shadowing is a pronunciation and fluency drill.
You are borrowing someone else's words, so it cannot replace the moment where you
have to find your own. Treat it as warm-up and polish for conversation, not a
substitute for it.

## The main types of shadowing

People use the word for several slightly different exercises. You will usually
move through them in this order with each new clip.

| Type | What you do | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Mumble (silent) shadowing | Move your lips or whisper along with the audio | First pass, catching the rhythm |
| Script shadowing | Shadow while reading the transcript | Getting every word right |
| Blind shadowing | Shadow with the transcript hidden | Real listening and real speed |
| Chorusing | Speak exactly with the audio, not behind it | Matching melody precisely |
| Delayed repetition | Pause after each sentence and repeat it | Beginners and hard material |

If full shadowing feels impossible at first, start with delayed repetition and
work toward speaking over the audio. That is normal, not a sign that the method
isn't for you.

## How to shadow: a step-by-step routine

Here is a routine that takes ten to fifteen minutes a day. You use the same
short clip for several days, then move on.

A few details make a big difference:

- **Loop, don't cover ground.** Ten minutes on the same ninety seconds beats ten
  minutes on a whole episode. The value is in the repetitions.
- **Stay with a clip for three to five days.** Move on when you can shadow it
  blind without stumbling, or when you are bored.
- **Speak at full volume.** Quiet shadowing trains quiet, hesitant speech.
  Arguelles shadowed outside for a reason.
- **Slow the audio if you must.** Most players let you drop to 0.75x. Go back
  to normal speed as soon as you can, because natural speed is the point.

## What materials should you use for shadowing?

The right material is short, clear, natural, and comes with a transcript. It
should also be something you would actually want to say. Shadowing a news
anchor gives you a news anchor's voice, which sounds odd in a café.

| Material | Level | Why it works | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learner podcasts with transcripts | Beginner to intermediate | Clear speech, transcripts included | Can be slower and more careful than real speech |
| Street interviews with subtitles (e.g. the Easy Languages channels on YouTube) | Intermediate | Real people, real speed, everyday topics | Background noise, many speakers |
| Audiobook plus the ebook | Intermediate to advanced | Long, clean, professional narration | Narration style, not conversation |
| Dialogue from shows or films with subtitles | Intermediate to advanced | Emotional, natural phrasing | Subtitles often differ from what is said |
| Textbook audio | Beginner | Matches what you are studying | Often stiff and unnatural |
| Voice messages from a native speaker | Any | Everyday language, a voice you know, you can ask for more | Needs a partner |

Two rules for choosing:

1. **Understand at least most of it before you start.** Shadowing is not the
   place to learn new material from scratch. Understanding comes first, as we
   explain in our guide to [comprehensible input](/comprehensible-input).
2. **Pick one speaker you like.** Copying the same voice for a few weeks builds
   a consistent accent faster than jumping between regions.

## Common shadowing mistakes

Most people who try shadowing and give up made one of these mistakes.

**Using audio that is too hard.** If you have to stop every few seconds, you are
not shadowing, you are deciphering. Drop a level.

**Using clips that are too long.** A twenty-minute episode means you hear each
sentence once. Cut it down to a minute or two.

**Copying the words but not the music.** Most of what makes speech sound foreign
is rhythm, stress and intonation rather than individual sounds. Exaggerate the
rises and falls, the pauses, the linked words.

**Never recording yourself.** You can't hear yourself accurately while
speaking. A thirty-second recording each session shows you what to fix.

**Shadowing on autopilot.** After a few days you can mouth a clip without
thinking about its meaning. That is the moment to move on or to switch to blind
shadowing.

**Skipping the step where you use the phrases.** A phrase you have shadowed fifty
times is ready to use. If you never say it to a person, it stays a party trick.
Put the best phrases on flashcards (see our [spaced repetition
guide](/spaced-repetition-language-learning)) and use them in your next
conversation.

The weakness of shadowing is that it has no feedback and no conversation. A

## A sample week of shadowing

If you want a starting plan, this fits into fifteen minutes a day:

- **Days 1 to 3:** one clip from a learner podcast. Full routine every day,
  recording on day 3.
- **Days 4 and 5:** a voice message from a partner, based on your own corrected
  sentences. Shadow it, send yours back.
- **Days 6 and 7:** a new clip, slightly harder, such as a street interview.
  Compare your day 7 recording with day 1.

Once the habit is in place, you can fold shadowing into a wider speaking
routine, like the 30-day plan in our [speaking guide](/how-to-improve-speaking-skills-in-a-foreign-language),
and use the phrases you learn to start [thinking in the

## FAQ

### Does shadowing really work?

It works well for what it trains: pronunciation, rhythm, intonation and the
speed at which you can produce familiar phrases. Researchers such as Shuhei
Kadota have studied it in classrooms, mostly with learners of English. It does
not teach you to build new sentences on your own, so combine it with
conversation.

### How long should I shadow each day?

Ten to fifteen minutes is enough for most people. Short daily sessions work
better than an occasional hour, because the goal is many repetitions of a small
amount of audio.

### Can beginners use the shadowing technique?

Yes, with easy material. Start with slow, clear audio such as textbook dialogues
or beginner podcasts, and use delayed repetition (pause, then repeat) until you
can keep up with the speaker.

### Should I shadow with or without the transcript?

Both, in that order. Shadow with the script first so you get every word right,
then hide it so you are really listening.

### What is the difference between shadowing and repeating?

When you repeat, you wait until the speaker finishes and then say the sentence.
When you shadow, you speak while they are still talking, slightly behind them,
which forces you to copy the sound directly instead of reconstructing it.
