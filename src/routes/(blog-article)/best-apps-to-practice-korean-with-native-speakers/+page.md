Korean rewards a language exchange more than most languages, because so much of it is about *how* you say something to *whom*. Every sentence carries a speech level, and the only way to get a feel for it is to talk to real people and have them tell you when you sound too stiff or too familiar. ## How to pick an app to practice Korean

**Learn Hangul before anything else.** Hangul has 14 basic consonants and 10 basic vowels, packed into one square block per syllable. Most learners can read it slowly within a week or two. Romanization is a crutch that stops working the moment a partner writes back in Korean, so an app is only as useful as your ability to read what arrives. Our [Korean alphabet chart](/tools/alphabet/korean) lays out every letter with its sound.

**Go where Korean speakers are.** An exchange works only if native Korean speakers who are learning your language use the same app. That favors large apps with a strong presence in East Asia.

**Check the reading aids.** Transliteration, in-chat translation and read-aloud matter far more for Korean than for Spanish or French in your first months.

**Plan for a long road.** The US Foreign Service Institute places Korean in its hardest category for English speakers, alongside Japanese, Chinese and Arabic (more in our post on [the easiest and hardest languages](/easiest-languages-to-learn-for-english-speakers)). Pick apps you will still enjoy opening a year from now.

## Language exchange apps for Korean

### HelloTalk

[HelloTalk](https://www.hellotalk.com) is based in Shenzhen, China, and according to its Wikipedia entry more than half of its users come from China, Japan, South Korea and the United States. That makes it the most practical place to find Korean partners, especially Korean speakers learning English.

- **Best for:** finding Korean partners quickly. Its transliteration tool turns Hangul into romanization, translation sits inside the chat, and **Moments** lets several native speakers correct a short post at once.
- **Keep in mind:** translation, transliteration and voice-to-text have daily caps on the free tier, and the free version shows ads; VIP lifts both. Lean on the romanization button for too long and it will slow your reading.

### Tandem

[Tandem](https://tandem.net) reviews new members and is built around one-to-one chats with text, voice messages and calls. It suits learners who want one or two regular Korean partners rather than a busy feed.

- **Best for:** a steady weekly exchange partner.
- **Keep in mind:** the free plan allows 3 translations a day, and the AI toolkit (grammar check, correction explanations) is part of Tandem Pro.

## Korean-specific tools worth adding

**[Naver Papago](https://papago.naver.com)** is a translator from Naver, the company behind South Korea's biggest search portal. It translates text, speech and photos, and many learners find it handles casual Korean well. Use it to check your sentence *before* you send it, then let your partner correct what Papago missed.

**[Naver Dictionary](https://dict.naver.com)** is a free Korean-English dictionary on the web, iOS and Android, with audio and plenty of example sentences. Example sentences show you which speech level a word usually appears in, which a bare translation never will.

**A Korean keyboard.** Add the Korean (2-set, or 두벌식) keyboard on your phone. Typing Hangul yourself, even slowly, earns much better corrections than romanization and trains your reading at the same time.

**Frequency lists.** The first few hundred words cover a large share of everyday Korean. Start with our [most common Korean words](/tools/most-common-words/korean), then see where you stand with the [Korean vocabulary test](/tools/vocabulary-test/korean).

## Speech levels in a Korean language exchange

This is where an exchange teaches what textbooks cannot.

- **존댓말 (jondaenmal), polite speech, is the default.** With anyone new, use the polite -요 (-yo) endings: 감사해요, 좋아요. You will almost never offend someone by being polite.
- **반말 (banmal), casual speech, comes by agreement.** Among friends of a similar age, Koreans often switch to banmal, but usually after one person asks. A common way to ask is 우리 말 놓을까요? (uri mal noeulkkayo?), roughly "Shall we speak casually?" Let your partner suggest it, or ask once you have been chatting for a while.
- **Age matters.** Korean partners may ask your age early. It is not nosiness: age helps decide who speaks how, and which family-style titles (형, 오빠, 누나, 언니) fit.
- **Formal -습니다 (-seumnida) is fine but stiff.** It is used in news, presentations and service settings. In a chat between peers, -요 is plenty.
- **A "correction" may be about register.** If your partner rewrites a sentence that was grammatically fine, ask whether it was too formal or too casual. That answer is worth more than a vocabulary list.

## Your first message in Korean

Keep it short and polite. Romanization follows the Revised Romanization of Korean.

> 안녕하세요! 저는 샘이에요. 한국어를 공부하고 있어요. 만나서 반가워요. 잘 부탁드려요.

(Annyeonghaseyo! Jeoneun Saem-ieyo. Hangugeoreul gongbuhago isseoyo. Mannaseo bangawoyo. Jal butakdeuryeoyo.)

In English: "Hello! I'm Sam. I'm studying Korean. Nice to meet you. I look forward to talking with you." 잘 부탁드려요 has no neat English equivalent; it is a polite way to close an introduction.

Add a line in English saying what you can help with. Then a few phrases you will use constantly:

- **감사합니다** (gamsahamnida): thank you.
- **다시 한 번 말해 주세요** (dasi han beon malhae juseyo): please say it once more.
- **천천히 말해 주세요** (cheoncheonhi malhae juseyo): please speak slowly.
- **틀린 거 있으면 고쳐 주세요** (teullin geo isseumyeon gochyeo juseyo): if anything is wrong, please correct it.

For topics beyond introductions, see [language exchange conversation topics](/language-exchange-conversation-topics), and for a clear structure for each session, [how to do a language exchange](/how-to-do-a-language-exchange).

## Tips for Korean learners on exchange apps

**Ask for corrections directly.** Many Korean partners are generous with corrections once asked, but may hold back at first to be polite. Send the phrase above in your first few messages.

**Use voice messages for pronunciation.** Korean sound changes mean words are often not pronounced the way they are spelled: 한국어 is said closer to "han-gu-geo". Ask your partner to send a short voice message with the text underneath, listen first, then read.

**Watch out for Konglish.** Words like 핸드폰 (haendeupon, "hand phone") for mobile phone look like English but are Korean. Your partner can tell you which ones are real Korean words and which only sound English.

**Keep a correction notebook.** Korean corrections are often a single particle (은/는 versus 이/가) or an ending. Write them down or turn them into flashcards the same day.

## FAQ

### What is the best app to practice Korean with native speakers?

HelloTalk is usually the easiest place to find Korean partners, because a large share of its community is in East Asia, including South Korea. Tandem is better for a calmer one-to-one exchange, LangX for corrections on every message with no ads, and italki if you want a paid teacher.

### Should I use 존댓말 or 반말 in a language exchange?

Start with polite -요 speech (존댓말). Switch to 반말 only when your partner suggests it or you have agreed to, which often happens between people of a similar age.

### Can I practice Korean speaking for free?

Yes. HelloTalk and Tandem include free calls with exchange partners. LangX supports voice messages but not live calls. Tutors on italki are paid, and Speak and Praktika are subscription apps with a trial or limited free use.

### Do I need to read Hangul before using an exchange app?

Not strictly, since translation and romanization help at first, but Hangul takes only days to learn and makes every conversation easier. Start with the [Korean alphabet](/tools/alphabet/korean) before your first message.
