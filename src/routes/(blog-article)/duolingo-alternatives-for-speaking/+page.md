If Duolingo has given you a streak but not a conversation, the fix is to add
something that makes you talk to a person: a **language exchange app** (LangX,
Tandem, HelloTalk) for free, regular practice with native speakers, or a
**tutor** (italki, Preply) for guided speaking with a professional. Around that,
**Pimsleur** trains you to answer out loud, **Babbel** and **Busuu** add
structure, **comprehensible input** like Dreaming Spanish builds your listening,
and **Anki** keeps the vocabulary from leaking out.

This guide is not "Duolingo is bad, delete it." Duolingo is very good at one job.
The trouble starts when you expect it to do a different one. Below: what
Duolingo does well, where it stops, and which alternative fits which goal. Every
product was checked as active at the time of writing (September 2026), and we
do not list prices because they change and vary by country.

## What Duolingo is genuinely good at

It is worth being fair here, because a lot of the "Duolingo alternatives" posts
on r/duolingo and elsewhere are written by people who are frustrated with it
rather than people who understand what it is for.

- **It gets you to show up.** Streaks, leagues, short lessons and the owl are
  engineered to make five minutes a day feel non-negotiable. For most learners,
  consistency is the hardest part, and Duolingo solves it better than anything
  else on this list.
- **It is a gentle on-ramp.** You can start a language with zero knowledge and
  no plan, and within a week you can read simple sentences.
- **It covers a lot of languages** with a free tier, so it is a low-risk way to
  find out whether you actually like a language before you invest in it.
- **It has started to address speaking.** Duolingo Max, its top subscription,
  includes Video Call with Lily, an AI character you talk to, and Roleplay
  scenarios with AI feedback. At the time of writing, Video Call is available
  only for some courses and platforms.

## Where Duolingo stops

The limits are structural, not a matter of effort:

- **Recognition is not production.** Most exercises ask you to recognize,
  arrange or complete a sentence someone else wrote. Speaking asks you to build
  one from nothing, in real time, which is a different skill.
- **Nobody interrupts you.** Real conversations include slang, accents, people
  talking fast, and questions you did not see coming. A lesson path is designed
  so that you are ready for the next step; a person is not.
- **AI conversation helps, but it is not a person.** An AI partner is patient
  and always available, which is useful. It will not tell you what people
  actually say in Madrid or Seoul, and it will never become a friend you want to
  keep talking to, which is what keeps people practicing for years.

So the question is not "what replaces Duolingo" but "what fills the speaking
gap." That depends on your goal, your budget and how much structure you want.

## For conversation practice: language exchange apps

A language exchange pairs you with someone who speaks the language you are
learning and wants to learn yours. You help each other, so it costs nothing but
your time. If the idea is new to you, start with
[what a language exchange is](/what-is-a-language-exchange) and
[how to do one well](/how-to-do-a-language-exchange).

### LangX

LangX is the app this blog belongs to, so weigh this section accordingly. What
it does differently:

- **Two-way matching.** You only see people who speak what you are learning
  *and* are learning what you speak, so every conversation is useful to both
  sides.
- **Corrections on any message, unlimited on every plan.** Tap a message to
  correct it. The part that teaches is never rationed.
- **Translation inside the chat**, voice and photo messages, read-aloud,
  and daily streaks, which will feel familiar if you are coming from Duolingo.
- **Open source (BSD-3) and no ads.** The code is on
  [GitHub](https://github.com/langx/langx).

The honest limits: the free plan lets you start 5 new conversations a day
(replies are unlimited) and translate 20 messages a day; the paid plans,
Fluent and Polyglot, raise those and add filters, as listed on the
[plans page](/pro). LangX is younger and its community is smaller than Tandem's
or HelloTalk's, and conversations happen in chat, with text, voice notes and
photos, rather than in scheduled video lessons. LangX Copilot, private AI
feedback, is not shipped yet.

### Tandem

Tandem is one of the best-known exchange apps. It supports text, voice messages
and audio and video calls, has a tutor section for paid lessons, and is often
praised for tighter profile moderation. A Pro subscription removes ads and adds
extra features. If you want video calls with exchange partners inside the same
app, Tandem is a strong choice. We compare the two in detail on
[LangX vs Tandem](/open-source-alternative-to-tandem).

### HelloTalk

HelloTalk feels more like a social network for learners: a public feed called
Moments where natives correct your posts, in-chat corrections and translation,
and live voice rooms. It is good for volume, meaning lots of people and lots of
short interactions. The flip side is more noise to filter. See
[LangX vs HelloTalk](/open-source-alternative-to-hellotalk) and our broader
roundup of the [best language exchange apps](/best-language-exchange-apps).

## For guided speaking: tutors

If you want someone whose job is to make you talk, and who will plan a lesson
around your mistakes, pay a tutor. This is the fastest route for exam
preparation, business language, or anyone who freezes up with strangers.

- **italki** is a marketplace of professional teachers and less formal community
  tutors across a very wide range of languages. At the time of writing you pay
  per lesson, with no subscription, which suits irregular schedules. See
  [LangX vs italki](/open-source-alternative-to-italki) for when an exchange is
  enough and when a teacher is worth it.
- **Preply** is also a tutor marketplace, but at the time of writing it sells
  lessons as a recurring subscription with a tutor you choose. That structure
  suits people who want a fixed weekly slot and some accountability.

Many learners combine the two approaches: a tutor once a week for structure,
and an exchange partner in between for free, unscripted practice.

## For speaking out loud on your commute: Pimsleur

Pimsleur is an audio course built around 30-minute lessons in which a recorded
voice asks you to say things and then gives you the answer. You speak from the
first lesson, and you can do it while walking or driving. It covers more than
50 languages. Pimsleur is excellent for pronunciation and for the reflex of
answering out loud. It is lighter on reading and on vocabulary breadth, and the
"conversation" is with a recording, so it is best paired with real people once
you have the basics.

## For structure: Babbel and Busuu

If what you liked about Duolingo was the structure, and what you disliked was
the feeling of not really understanding the grammar, these two are the closest
swaps.

- **Babbel** offers lessons built around practical dialogues with clear grammar
  explanations. It also runs Babbel Live, small live online classes with a
  teacher, in a handful of languages. The live classes are the part that gets
  you speaking.
- **Busuu** combines a course with a community feature where native speakers
  review your written and spoken exercises. Busuu, owned by Chegg since 2022,
  has also added AI conversation practice in its mobile apps. It is a good
  middle ground between a course and an exchange. We compare it with LangX on
  [LangX vs Busuu](/open-source-alternative-to-busuu).

## For listening first: comprehensible input

Comprehensible input means watching or listening to material that you can
mostly understand, lots of it. **Dreaming Spanish**, now part of a platform
called Dreaming that also covers French, is the best-known example: thousands
of videos graded by level, with no English and no exercises. Some of it is on
YouTube; a premium subscription unlocks the full library.

This one deserves a caveat in a post about speaking. Dreaming recommends that
you hold off on speaking until you have built up a lot of listening, on the
theory that your pronunciation and instincts come out better. Many learners
love it; others want to talk sooner. Either way, heavy listening makes every
conversation easier, because most of what stops you in a real chat is not
knowing what the other person just said.

## For vocabulary that sticks: Anki

Anki is a free, open-source flashcard app that uses spaced repetition to show
you each card just before you would forget it. It is plain and takes some setup,
but it is the most reliable way to keep thousands of words. In February 2026
its creator, Damien Elmes, handed stewardship of the project to the team
behind AnkiHub, who committed to keeping it open source.

A good starting deck is simply the most frequent words of your language. Our
free [most common words lists](/tools/most-common-words) cover 53 languages,
ranked by frequency with English meanings, and every list downloads in a format
that imports into Anki. If you want to know where to start, the
[vocabulary tests](/tools/vocabulary-test) estimate how much of a language's
everyday vocabulary you already know in about two minutes. For how many words
you actually need, see
[how many words you need to be fluent](/how-many-words-do-you-need-to-be-fluent).

## How to combine them: a simple weekly plan

You do not need all of these. A realistic setup for someone with 30 to 45
minutes a day:

1. **Keep your Duolingo streak** if it is what gets you to open a language app
   every day. Treat it as a warm-up, not the whole workout.
2. **Add 10 minutes of flashcards** with the most common words.
3. **Message one or two exchange partners** most days. Short, regular chats beat
   one long call a month. Send voice notes, not just text.
4. **Once a week, do something harder:** a tutor lesson, a live class, or a
   voice or video call with a partner you have gotten to know.
5. **Listen to something every day**: comprehensible input, a podcast or a
   show you half understand.

If speaking to strangers is the part you dread, you are not alone; our
[introvert's guide to learning a language](/mastering-a-new-language-an-introverts-guide)
is written for exactly that.

## Which Duolingo alternative should you pick?

- **You want free, regular speaking practice:** a language exchange app. LangX
  if you want two-way matching and unlimited corrections without ads; Tandem if
  in-app video calls matter most; HelloTalk if you like a busy social feed.
- **You want fast, guided progress and can pay:** a tutor on italki or Preply.
- **You want to talk from day one but alone:** Pimsleur.
- **You want a better-explained course:** Babbel or Busuu.
- **Your listening is the bottleneck:** comprehensible input.
- **You keep forgetting words:** Anki plus a frequency list.

## FAQ

### Is Duolingo enough to become fluent?

On its own, for most people, no. Duolingo is very good for building a daily
habit and a foundation of vocabulary and grammar, but fluency means holding
unscripted conversations, and that needs practice with people. Use Duolingo as
one part of your routine and add a speaking source.

### What is the best free Duolingo alternative for speaking?

A language exchange app is the best free option for speaking with real people,
because you trade practice with a native speaker instead of paying. LangX,
Tandem and HelloTalk all have free plans; paid tiers add features on each.

### Is Duolingo's AI video call a replacement for a speaking partner?

It is a useful rehearsal space: always available, never impatient, and good for
losing the fear of speaking. It is not a replacement for a person, who brings
real accents, slang, unexpected questions and a relationship that keeps you
coming back.

### Can I use Duolingo and a language exchange app together?

Yes, and it is a good combination. Duolingo gives you the daily structure and
new material; an exchange partner gives you a place to use it. Try to bring one
thing you learned in a lesson into a real conversation each day.

### Should I pay for a tutor or use a language exchange?

If you have a deadline, an exam, or you freeze with strangers, a tutor is worth
it. If you want lots of practice over a long time, an exchange is free and
unlimited. Many learners use both: a weekly lesson for structure and an exchange
partner for everything in between.
