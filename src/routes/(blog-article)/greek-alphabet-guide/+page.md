The Greek alphabet has 24 letters, from alpha (Α α) to omega (Ω ω): 7 vowels and 17 consonants. You probably know many of them from math and science, but modern Greek says several of them differently from the names English speakers use: beta is *vita* and sounds like v, delta sounds like the th in "this", and eta, iota and upsilon all sound like the ee in "see". Learn the 24 letters, then the letter pairs that make one sound, then the stress mark, and you can read Greek aloud.

This guide uses the letters, names and sounds from our free [Greek alphabet chart](/tools/alphabet/greek), and every practice word comes from our list of the [10,000 most common Greek words](/tools/most-common-words/greek), with its rank on that list.

## What kind of script is Greek?

Greek is an alphabet in the plainest sense: vowels and consonants are equal letters, written left to right, with capitals and small letters, just like English. That's no coincidence. The Greeks adapted their alphabet from the Phoenician script around the 8th century BC, and they turned some Phoenician consonant letters they didn't need into vowels. The Latin alphabet was later built from a Greek alphabet by way of the Etruscans, and Cyrillic was created largely from Greek letters. Our alphabet data describes Greek as the oldest alphabet still in use that writes vowels as letters in their own right.

So an English speaker already has a head start. Α, Β, Ε, Ζ, Ι, Κ, Μ, Ν, Ο, Τ and others look familiar. The catch is that some familiar shapes stand for different sounds: Η is an ee sound, Ρ is an r, Χ is a throaty h, and small ν is an n, not a v.

Sigma is the only letter with two small forms. Write σ at the start or in the middle of a word and ς at the very end, as in σας (your, #33), which uses both.

## Modern Greek sounds versus the names you know

Most English speakers meet Greek letters in a math or physics class: π for the circle, Σ for a sum, Δ for a change, μ for micro, λ for wavelength, Ω for ohms. The names used there, and the way English says them, come from how Western scholars pronounced Ancient Greek. Modern Greek has moved on, so several letters sound different today.

| Letter | How English says the name | Ancient sound | Modern sound |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| β | BAY-tuh | b | v |
| γ | GAM-uh | g | soft g, or y |
| δ | DEL-tuh | d | th in this |
| η | AY-tuh | a long e | ee |
| θ | THAY-tuh | t with a puff of air | th in thin |
| υ | UP-si-lon | like French u | ee |
| φ | fye or fee | p with a puff of air | f |
| χ | kye | k with a puff of air | ch in loch |

The names change too. A Greek says *pi* as "pee", *mu* as "mee", *nu* as "nee", *chi* as a throaty "hee" and *psi* as "psee". If you ask for "the letter beta" in Athens, people will understand you, but *vita* is what they say.

<Callout type="info">For reading modern Greek, forget the math pronunciation. When you see β, say v; when you see δ, say the th in "this".</Callout>

## Greek vowels: seven letters, five sounds

Greek has seven vowel letters, α, ε, η, ι, ο, υ and ω, but modern Greek has only five vowel sounds:

| Sound | Written as |
| --- | --- |
| a in father | α |
| e in bet | ε, αι |
| ee in see | ι, η, υ, ει, οι |
| o in got | ο, ω |
| oo in boot | ου |

This is good news for reading and bad news for spelling. When you read, every way of writing *ee* says *ee*. When you write a word you've only heard, you have to know which one it uses, which is why spelling Greek takes longer than reading it.

## Greek digraphs: two letters, one sound

The most useful thing to learn after the 24 letters is the list of letter pairs that make a single sound. Each example here is from our common words list.

### Vowel pairs

| Pair | Sounds like | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ου | oo in boot | μου (of me, #7), που (that, #15) |
| αι | e in bet | και (and, #6), ναι (yes, #26) |
| ει | ee in see | εκεί (there, #70) |
| οι | ee in see | οικογένεια (family, #408) |
| αυ | av, or af before voiceless consonants such as κ, π, τ, χ | αύριο (tomorrow, #270) |
| ευ | ev, or ef before voiceless consonants such as κ, π, τ, χ | ευχαριστώ (thank you, #75) |

Note that ναι, the Greek word for "yes", is said *ne*, which sounds a lot like "no" to English ears. And όχι, "no", is said *O-hee*.

### Consonant pairs

| Pair | Sounds like | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| μπ | b at the start of a word, b or mb inside one | μπορώ (I can, #64), μπαμπάς (dad, #470) |
| ντ | d at the start of a word, d or nd inside one | ντομάτα (tomato, #9054), εντάξει (OK, #48) |
| γκ | g at the start of a word, g or ng inside one | γκρι (grey, #4374) |
| γγ | ng in finger | |
| τσ | ts in cats | τσάι (tea, #1010) |
| τζ | dz in adze | |

Greek has no single letter for b, d or hard g, since β, δ and γ changed their sounds long ago. That's why words borrowed from other languages use μπ, ντ and γκ: μπύρα for beer, ντομάτα for tomato.

## The stress mark: tonos

Every Greek word of two or more syllables carries one accent mark, the **tonos** (´), on the vowel you stress. Since 1982 Greek has used this single mark in everyday writing; the older system had several accents and breathing marks, which you'll still see in church books and some older texts.

The tonos is not decoration. It tells you how to say the word, and some words differ only in where it falls:

| With the stress here | Meaning | With the stress there | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| πότε (#299) | when | ποτέ (#82) | ever |

Most one-syllable words have no tonos. A few carry one anyway to tell them apart from a word spelled the same way: πού (where, #104) versus που (that, #15), πώς (how, #77) versus πως (that, #45), and ή (or, #76) versus the article η.

### The diaeresis

A second mark, two dots (¨), tells you that two vowels that would normally form a pair are said separately. In μαϊμού (monkey, #3620), the αϊ is *a-ee*, not *e*: ma-ee-MOO. In φαΐ (food, #4300) the two dots sit on top of the accent: fa-EE.

One more surprise: Greek uses **;** as its question mark. Τι; means "What?"

Start with και, ναι and όχι: three everyday words, each one a small trap for English speakers. Then σπίτι and νερό, which look scary but use only the plainest sounds. By θάλασσα and ψωμί you're reading letters that don't exist in English.

## FAQ

### How many letters are in the Greek alphabet?

24: seven vowels (α, ε, η, ι, ο, υ, ω) and 17 consonants. Sigma has an extra small form, ς, used only at the end of a word.

### Why is beta pronounced vita in Greek?

The sound changed over time. In Ancient Greek β was a b; in modern Greek it is a v, and the letter's name changed with it. The English name "beta" comes from the older pronunciation.

### How do you write b and d in Greek?

With pairs of letters: μπ for b and ντ for d, as in μπορώ (I can) and ντομάτα (tomato). A hard g is written γκ.

### What is the accent mark in Greek called?

The tonos (´). It marks the stressed vowel in every word of more than one syllable, and it can change the meaning, as in πότε (when) and ποτέ (ever).

### Is the Greek alphabet hard to learn?

Most learners find it quick to read, because it has only 24 letters and many look like English ones. Spelling takes longer, since the ee sound alone has five common spellings.
