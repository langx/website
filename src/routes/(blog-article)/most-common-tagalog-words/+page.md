The most common Tagalog words are small ones: *ang* and *ng*, which mark who does what in a sentence, *sa* (to, for), *na* (already; now), *ko* (my), *hindi* (no; not) and *ako* (I). After them come the words you'd expect in any first conversation: *oo* (yes), *salamat* (thank you), *bakit* (why), *bahay* (house), *tubig* (water).

This starter list takes the top words from our free [Tagalog word list](/tools/most-common-words/tagalog) of 3,068 words, groups them so they're easier to learn, and explains the particles that make Tagalog sound like Tagalog.

## Where this list comes from

The ranking comes from the OpenSubtitles 2018 frequency lists (via the FrequencyWords project), which count how often each word appears in film and TV subtitles. That makes it a list of spoken, everyday Tagalog rather than textbook Tagalog. English meanings come from Wiktionary, and any word without a Tagalog Wiktionary entry was dropped, which filters out names and stray English. What's left is 3,068 words, all with a meaning. The data is CC BY-SA 4.0; the example sentences below are from Tatoeba.

The meanings are matched automatically, so now and then the list picks a rare sense of a common word. Where that happened in the rows below, we left the row out rather than print a misleading meaning, and we say which ones.

## Tagalog particles: the small words that do a lot

More than a third of the top 30 are particles or markers. The [Tagalog word list page](/tools/most-common-words/tagalog) sums up why: Tagalog marks who is doing what with small particles rather than word order, and the verb changes shape to say which part of the sentence is in focus. You can't skip these words, so it is worth meeting them early. Here they are with their listed meanings and an example sentence from the list.

| Rank | Particle | Listed meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 13 | lang | only; just | *Madali lang!* (Piece of cake!) |
| 14 | ba | marks a sentence as a question | *Aalis ka ba?* (Are you leaving?) |
| 29 | nga | emphasizes affirmation or confirmation | *Tama ka nga.* (You were right.) |
| 36 | naman | used after predicate adjectives | *Ayan na naman!* (Oh no, not again!) |
| 85 | po | marks respect toward the person addressed | *Pakiulit po.* (Pardon me?) |
| 114 | pala | sudden realization; surprise | *Naghintay pala si Tom.* (Tom apparently waited.) |
| 119 | muna | for a while; for the time being | *Sumilong ka muna.* (Take shelter first.) |

A few that deserve a note:

- **Ba turns a statement into a question.** *Aalis ka* is "you're leaving"; add *ba* and it's "are you leaving?" It usually sits right after the first word or phrase.
- **Po is politeness.** It marks respect toward the person you're speaking to, so you'll use it with anyone older or anyone you don't know. Dropping it where it's expected sounds abrupt.
- **Lang is "only" or "just".** It is the short, everyday form of *lamang* (#134, same meaning).
- **Ang, ng, sa, si and ni are markers.** *Ang* and *ng* mark the roles in the sentence, *si* (#19) and *ni* (#32, "of; possessive particle") do the same job before people's names, and *sa* covers "to" and "for". Learn them as a set.

## "Merely", "only" and "just" in Tagalog

People often look up "merely" in Tagalog, and the data adds a useful caveat. Our list has *sukat* (#765) with the meaning "just; merely; simply". But the everyday way to say "only" or "just" is *lang* (#13) or its longer form *lamang* (#134). *Sukat* is far less common, and the one example sentence the list has for it uses a different sense entirely: *Ito ang tamang sukat*, "It's the right size."

So if you want to say "it's merely a joke" or "just one", reach for *lang*. See [merely](/tools/say/merely) and [just](/tools/say/just) in other languages.

## Tagalog words you may know from Spanish

The word list page notes that centuries of Spanish rule left a large loan vocabulary in Tagalog. Our [similar words tool](/tools/similar) measures a strict version of that: words spelled identically **and** carrying the same meaning in both languages, within the 2,000 commonest words of each.

The 21 include *pero* (but), *o* (or), *minuto* (minute), *libro* (book), *grupo* (group), *hotel*, *mesa* (table), *papel* (paper), *tren* (train), *planeta* (planet), *isla* (island) and *imposible* (impossible). See all of them on [Spanish and Tagalog](/tools/similar/spanish-and-tagalog).

The strict test misses a lot. Scan the list above and you'll find *mundo*, *problema* and *trabaho*, which look Spanish but didn't make the identical-pairs list because the spelling or the recorded meaning differs slightly, or because one side falls outside the top 2,000. If you know Spanish, you will recognize more Tagalog nouns than you might expect. The grammar, particles and verbs are another matter.

## FAQ

### What is the most common word in Tagalog?

*Ang*, the marker for the doer or focus of the sentence. It is followed by *sa* (to, for), *na* (already; now) and *ng*.

### How do you say "merely" in Tagalog?

Our list has *sukat* ("just; merely; simply", #765), but in everyday speech *lang* (#13) or *lamang* (#134), both meaning "only; just", are far more common. Use *lang*.

### What does "po" mean in Tagalog?

*Po* marks respect toward the person you're addressing. It is the 85th most common word on our list, and you add it to sentences like *salamat po* (thank you) when speaking to someone older or someone you don't know.

### How many Tagalog words should I learn first?

Start with the top 100 on the list, particles included. They cover the small words you'll hear in almost every sentence. Then work down the list and check your level with the vocabulary test.

### Does Tagalog use Spanish words?

Yes, many. By our strict test, 21 of the 2,000 most common words are spelled the same and mean the same in both, such as *pero*, *mesa* and *libro*, and many more are close, such as *trabaho* and *problema*.
