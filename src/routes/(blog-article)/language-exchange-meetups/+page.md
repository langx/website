To find a language exchange meetup near you, search [Meetup](https://www.meetup.com) for "language exchange" plus your city, check whether [Mundo Lingo](https://mundolingo.org) or a Polyglot Club runs a weekly night where you live, ask your university's language center about tandem programs, and look at language exchange events on Couchsurfing. Most meetups are free: you show up at a bar or café, wear a sticker or name tag with your languages, and talk to whoever speaks what you are learning.

One evening at a meetup can give you more speaking time than a week of apps. This guide covers where meetups are listed, what happens at one, your first event, and how to keep practicing in between. Details about each organization are from their own sites at the time of writing (September 2026), and schedules change, so always check the listing before you go.

## Where to find a language exchange meetup

| Where | What it is | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Meetup.com | Listings for independent language exchange groups | Big cities, lots of choice |
| Mundo Lingo | Free weekly language socials with flag stickers | Meeting many people in one night |
| Polyglot Clubs | Recurring multilingual socials in several cities | Regular, lively events |
| University tandem programs | One-to-one partner matching each semester | Students; steady partners |
| Couchsurfing events and Hangouts | Traveler and local meetups, including language nights | Travelers and new arrivals |
| Libraries and cultural institutes | Conversation clubs and language cafés | Calm, structured practice |

### Meetup.com

[Meetup](https://www.meetup.com) is the largest general listing. Its [language exchange topic page](https://www.meetup.com/topics/language-exchange/) lists more than 1,300 groups at the time of writing, from multilingual socials in capitals to small "practice Dutch" or "English-Japanese" groups. Search "language exchange," "language café" or your target language plus your city.

A few things to check on any group:

- **When the last event was.** Groups linger long after they stop meeting. Look for events in the past month.
- **Recent attendee comments and photos.** They tell you the size and feel of the night.
- **Whether it is really a language exchange.** Some "international" groups are mostly social events where English is the only language spoken. That is fine, but not if you came to practice Italian.

Joining a group is usually free, though some organizers charge a small fee for particular events or expect you to buy a drink at the venue.

### Mundo Lingo

[Mundo Lingo](https://mundolingo.org) started in Buenos Aires in 2011 and runs free weekly language socials in bars and cafés. When you arrive, you get flag stickers: your native language at the top, and the languages you speak or are learning below. You then walk up to anyone whose flags match yours. Its rule of thumb is "stand to mingle, sit to quit": standing means you are open to conversation, sitting means you are taking a break.

There is no registration or ticket, and Mundo Lingo says it never charges entry; buying a drink supports the venue. At the time of writing its [city directory](https://mundolingo.org/cities) lists active chapters in places including Buenos Aires, São Paulo, Rio de Janeiro, New York City, San Francisco, Montreal, London, Paris, Madrid, Lisbon, Stockholm, Bangkok and Melbourne. Check the directory for your city and the current night.

### Polyglot Clubs and other recurring socials

Many cities have a long-running multilingual social under its own name. Polyglot Club groups are a common example: [Polyglot Club Paris](https://www.meetup.com/polyglotclub-paris/), for instance, lists free weekly events on Meetup. The format is similar to Mundo Lingo: a bar, a crowd, and tables or badges organized by language. Search Meetup and local listings for "polyglot," "language café" or "tandem" in your city.

### University tandem programs

If you are a student, your university may already run the best meetup of all: a **tandem program** that pairs you with a student who speaks the language you are learning and wants to learn yours. Examples include the Language Resource Center at [WU Vienna](https://www.wu.ac.at/en/lrc/tandem-learning), the [TUM Language Center](https://www.sprachenzentrum.tum.de/en/sz/tandem-learning/) in Munich and [Wilfrid Laurier University](https://students.wlu.ca/academics/global-engagement-and-exchanges/international-at-home/language-sharing-programs.html) in Canada, all of which list registration dates for the fall 2026 term at the time of writing. Programs like these are usually free for students (sometimes staff too), you register at the start of term, and there is often a kick-off event where you meet your partner.

Tandem partners from a program tend to be motivated and reliable, because both of you signed up for it. Check your language center or international office; registration windows are short.

### Couchsurfing events and Hangouts

[Couchsurfing](https://www.couchsurfing.com) is known for hosting travelers, but its events section has long listed language exchange nights posted by members in many cities, often weekly. In August 2026 Couchsurfing also brought back **Group Hangouts** in its mobile app, letting you see who is nearby and create or join a small meetup. It is best suited to travelers and people new to a city. Couchsurfing has charged for membership since 2020, so check what your account can use before relying on it.

### Libraries, cultural institutes and language cafés

Public libraries, community centers and cultural institutes often host free conversation clubs, and some cafés run regular "language café" evenings. They are calmer than a bar night, a good first step if crowds put you off. Ask at your local library or check the events page of the cultural institute for your target language.

## What happens at a language exchange meetup?

Formats vary, but most follow one of three patterns:

- **Open mingle.** Everyone wears a sticker, flag or name tag showing their languages, and you walk up to people. This is the Mundo Lingo model, and the most common.
- **Language tables.** Tables are labeled by language, and you sit at the one you want to practice, moving between them during the night.
- **Timed rotations.** Pairs talk for a set time, often split half in each language, then rotate, a bit like speed dating for languages.

Expect short conversations with lots of people rather than one long one. You will repeat your introduction many times, which is actually great practice. Levels are mixed: you will meet complete beginners and near-native speakers in the same room. And you will probably speak English more than you plan to, because it is often the shared language. Steering back to your target language politely is part of the skill.

## Tips for your first language exchange meetup

### Prepare your introduction

Practice a 30-second introduction in your target language: name, where you are from, what you do, why you are learning. You will use it all night. Knowing a few key phrases helps too; try [how to say hello](/tools/say/hello), [thanks](/tools/say/thanks) and [sorry](/tools/say/sorry) in your language if you are just starting out.

### Have a few questions ready

Conversations stall when you run out of questions, not words. Prepare five: where they learned the language, their favorite local food, a place you should visit, what they do for fun, a word they love. Our list of [100 conversation topics by level](/language-exchange-conversation-topics) has plenty more.

### Be clear about the exchange

"Can we do ten minutes in Spanish and then ten in English?" is a perfectly normal thing to say. Most people will be glad you suggested it.

### Bring a way to swap contacts

When you meet someone you enjoy talking with, suggest keeping in touch to practice. Exchanging usernames on a language app is less personal than a phone number.

### Stay safe

Meetups are public and generally friendly, but use common sense: watch your drink, keep your address to yourself, and tell an organizer if someone is bothering people.

## How to combine meetups with an app between events

A weekly meetup gives you maybe an hour or two of real speaking. That is excellent, but it is not enough on its own, and the gap between events is where people forget what they learned. The best results come from pairing the two:

- **Before the event**, spend a few minutes on the words you will need. The [most common words in Spanish](/tools/most-common-words/spanish), [French](/tools/most-common-words/french) or [German](/tools/most-common-words/german) cover a large share of everyday conversation.
- **At the event**, note the phrases you did not know how to say.

## FAQ

### How do I find a language exchange near me?

Search Meetup for "language exchange" plus your city, check Mundo Lingo's city directory, look at Couchsurfing events, and ask your university's language center or local library. In big cities you will usually find something every week.

### Are language exchange meetups free?

Most are. Mundo Lingo says it never charges entry, and many Meetup groups are free to attend, though you are usually expected to buy a drink at the venue. Some organizers charge a small fee for particular events.

### Do I need to be good at the language to go?

No. Meetups are mixed-level, and beginners are common. Prepare a short introduction and a few questions, and people will slow down for you.

### What if there is no language exchange meetup in my city?

Start with online options: a language exchange app for one-to-one practice and a [language exchange Discord server](/language-exchange-discord-servers) for group voice chat. If you want an in-person group, Mundo Lingo invites people to start a chapter, and anyone can create a Meetup group.

### How often should I go to language exchange meetups?

Once a week is a good rhythm if you practice a little every day in between. The meetup gives you speaking time; daily messages keep the words from fading.
