The fastest way to find a language exchange partner online is to join a language exchange app or community, write a specific profile, and send short, personal first messages to several people at once. Expect only some of them to reply, and keep the ones who show up consistently.

Finding someone is the easy part. Finding someone who is still talking to you in a month takes a little more thought, and that is what most of this guide is about: where to look, what to write, what to avoid, and how to keep an exchange alive once it starts. If you are new to the idea, start with [what a language exchange is and how it works](/what-is-a-language-exchange).

## Where can you find a language exchange partner?

There is no single best place. Each has a different kind of person on it, and the best results usually come from trying two at once.

| Where | Good for | Downsides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Language exchange apps | Matching by language, built-in correction and translation tools | Lots of inactive or unfocused users on some apps |
| Reddit (r/language_exchange) | Clear "offering / seeking" posts, people who took time to write | Slower, and you move to another app to chat |
| Discord servers | Voice channels, communities built around one language pair | Busy, and can feel chaotic at first |
| Local meetups | Face-to-face practice, meeting many speakers in one night | Depends on your city, short conversations |
| Universities and libraries | Structured programs, motivated partners | Often limited to students or residents |

### Language exchange apps

Apps are where most people start, because they handle the matching for you. You set the languages you speak and the ones you are learning, and the app shows you people on the other side of that trade. The best-known include [Tandem](https://tandem.net) and [HelloTalk](https://www.hellotalk.com), which both offer text, voice and correction tools, and there are smaller options for specific needs. We compared them all honestly, including where competitors do better than us, in [the best language exchange apps](/best-language-exchange-apps), and the two giants head-to-head in [Tandem vs HelloTalk](/tandem-vs-hellotalk).

[LangX](/) is our own app. Its matching runs in both directions, so everyone you see speaks the language you are learning *and* is learning one you speak, which cuts down on one-sided conversations. The free plan allows 5 new conversations a day, with unlimited replies and unlimited corrections. It is also open source, if that matters to you.

### Reddit: r/language_exchange

The subreddit r/language_exchange is a long-running board where people post what they offer and what they seek, usually in a title like "Offering: English | Seeking: French." Two ways to use it:

- **Reply to posts** that match your pair. Mention something specific from their post so it does not look copy-pasted.
- **Write your own post** with your languages and levels in the title, then a few lines about you, your time zone and whether you want text, voice or video.

Read the subreddit's rules before you post. Once you connect, you will usually move to an app or a messaging service to actually chat.

### Discord servers

Discord is excellent for voice practice. Many servers are built around a single language pair, with text channels for each language and voice channels where you can drop in. A well-known example is the English-Japanese Language Exchange server (discord.gg/japanese). To find others, search Discord's server discovery or a server list such as [Disboard](https://disboard.org) for "language exchange" plus your target language.

Tip: lurk for a day, then post a short introduction in the right channel. People are far more likely to invite you to a voice chat after they have seen you around.

### Local meetups

If you live in a city, check [Meetup](https://www.meetup.com) for "language exchange" groups, and look for recurring language events in bars and cafés. [Mundo Lingo](https://mundolingo.org), for example, runs free weekly language socials in many cities, where people wear flag stickers for the languages they speak. Meetups are great for meeting many speakers in one evening; turn the best conversations into a regular one-to-one exchange.

### Universities, libraries and cultural institutes

Many universities run tandem programs that pair local and international students each semester. Public libraries and cultural institutes often host conversation clubs as well. If you are a student, check your language center first: these partners tend to be motivated and reliable.

## How to write a language exchange profile that gets replies

Most profiles say "Hi, I want to learn English, message me." They get ignored. A good profile answers the three questions every potential partner has: what can you offer me, what do you want, and would I enjoy talking to you?

Include:

- **Your languages and honest levels.** "Native Spanish, fluent English, learning German (A2)."
- **What you want from an exchange.** Text, voice notes or calls? Casual chat or exam practice?
- **Two or three specific interests.** "Bouldering, Korean cooking, 90s hip-hop" beats "music, travel, food."
- **Your time zone or rough availability.**
- **One line in your target language**, even with mistakes. It shows effort and gives people an easy first correction.

### Profile template

> Hi! I'm Marta, a nurse from Valencia. Native Spanish, learning English (B1) and a little Japanese.
> I'm looking for a text and voice-note exchange, a few messages a day. I'm happy to correct your Spanish in as much detail as you like.
> I love hiking, horror films and trying to bake bread (badly).
> I'm usually free evenings, Central European Time.
> English: I want to speak more natural and stop translating in my head!

That last line has a small mistake in it, and that is fine. A native speaker will enjoy offering "I want to speak more naturally."

## How to write a first message that gets a reply

The best first messages are short, specific and easy to answer. They prove you read the profile, state the exchange clearly, and end with a question.

### First message templates

**Casual text exchange:**

> Hi Kenji! I saw you're learning English and into bouldering — I just started climbing too. I'm learning Japanese (beginner, about 6 months). Would you like to do a text exchange? We could alternate: one message in Japanese, one in English, and correct each other. What got you into climbing?

**Voice or video exchange:**

> Hi Camille, I'm Sam from Toronto. Native English, learning French (B1). Your profile says you want speaking practice before an interview — I'd be happy to help with that. Would you like to try a 40-minute call this weekend, 20 minutes in each language? I'm free Saturday morning my time (EST).

**Written in your target language** (even imperfectly):

> Hola Lucía, soy Emma. Estoy aprendiendo español desde hace un año. Me gusta mucho la comida mexicana y veo que tú también cocinas. ¿Quieres practicar juntas? Yo te ayudo con el inglés.

What to avoid in a first message:

- Just "hi" or "hello." It gives the other person all the work.
- Compliments on appearance. On most exchange apps, that reads as dating and gets you blocked.
- Asking for their phone number, Instagram or another app in the first few messages.
- A wall of text. Three to five lines is plenty.

Send it to several people. A low reply rate is normal and not a verdict on you; people are busy, inactive or already have enough partners.

## Red flags and staying safe

Most people in language exchange communities are exactly who they say they are. A few are not, and the warning signs are consistent:

- **They never use their target language** and never ask about yours. They may be after something other than a language exchange.
- **They push to move off the platform quickly**, to a private messenger, before you know them.
- **They make romantic or sexual comments** after you have made clear you are there to learn.
- **They bring up money**: investments, crypto, gifts, emergencies, "a great opportunity." This is the classic shape of a scam. End the conversation.
- **They ask for personal details** such as your address, workplace or financial information.
- **Their story does not add up**, or their photos look like stock images.

<Callout type="warning">
Never send money, gift cards or crypto to someone you met through a language exchange, whatever the reason. Keep chats inside the app until you trust someone, use the block and report buttons freely, and if you meet in person, choose a public place and tell a friend where you will be.
</Callout>

## How to keep a language exchange partner

Most exchanges die in the first two weeks, not because anyone did anything wrong, but because the conversation ran out of steam. These habits keep it going.

### Agree on the rules early

In the first few messages, settle the basics: which language when, how you want corrections (every mistake, or only the big ones), and how often you will talk. A clear 50/50 split is the single biggest factor in exchanges lasting, because both people feel they are getting something. We go deeper on this in [How to Do a Language Exchange: 12 Rules That Make It Work](/how-to-do-a-language-exchange).

### Give more than you take

Correct their messages carefully, explain things when they ask, and be patient. The partners who are generous teachers are the ones people want to keep talking to. On LangX, for example, you can hold any message to correct it, and corrections are unlimited on every plan, so there is no reason to hold back.

### Always leave a question open

Ending your message with a question gives your partner an easy reason to reply. If you run out of ideas, pull from our [100 conversation topics by level](/language-exchange-conversation-topics).

### Mix up the format

Text every day can get repetitive. Add a voice note, send a photo of your lunch or your street, or share a song and ask what they think of the lyrics. Voice notes are also the easiest way to practice pronunciation without scheduling a call.

### Keep a small, steady rhythm

A few messages a day beats a burst of long messages followed by silence. If you like streaks, use them; small daily habits are what make a language stick, as we explain in [why consistent practice is key](/why-consistent-practice-is-key-to-language-learning-success).

### Let it end gracefully

Some exchanges run their course. A short "I've really enjoyed this, but I don't have much time for a while. Good luck with your English!" is kinder than disappearing, and it leaves the door open.

## FAQ

### Where is the best place to find a language exchange partner?

A language exchange app is the quickest start because it matches you by language automatically. Combine it with one community, such as r/language_exchange or a Discord server for your language pair, to meet people who put more effort into their posts.

### How many language exchange partners should I have?

Start by messaging five to ten people, expect a few to reply, and keep the two or three you enjoy most. That is enough variety without spreading yourself too thin.

### Why do my language exchange messages get no replies?

Usually because the first message is too generic ("hi, want to practice?"), too long, or the profile is empty. Mention something specific from their profile, state the exchange clearly, and end with a question.

### Is it safe to do a language exchange with strangers online?

It is generally safe if you keep chats on the platform until you trust someone, never share financial or home details, and leave any conversation that turns to money or romance you did not ask for.

### Can I find a language exchange partner for free?

Yes. Reddit, Discord servers, many meetups and university programs cost nothing, and most language exchange apps, including LangX, have a free plan.
