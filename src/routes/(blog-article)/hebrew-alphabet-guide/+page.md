The Hebrew alphabet has 22 letters, written from right to left. It is an abjad: the letters are consonants, and the vowels are optional dots and dashes called niqqud, which most everyday Hebrew leaves out. Five letters take a different shape at the end of a word, a handful look confusingly alike, and three change their sound depending on a dot inside them. Learn those four things and you can sound out real Hebrew words.

This guide uses the letters, names and sounds from our free [Hebrew alphabet chart](/tools/alphabet/hebrew), and every practice word comes from our list of the [6,856 most common Hebrew words](/tools/most-common-words/hebrew), with its rank on that list.

## What kind of script is Hebrew?

Hebrew is an **abjad**: its letters write consonants, and a reader supplies most of the vowels. Four letters do double duty as vowel helpers. Vav (ו) can stand for *o* or *u*, yod (י) for *i*, and alef (א) and ayin (ע) are silent letters that carry a vowel, which is why so many Hebrew words seem to start with one.

It runs right to left, and it has no capital letters. Unlike Arabic, which is also an abjad, printed Hebrew letters stand apart instead of joining, so each letter has one shape, apart from the five final forms. The same alphabet is used for Yiddish and Ladino. Handwritten Hebrew uses a cursive style whose shapes differ quite a lot from print, so learn the printed letters first and the cursive later.

If you know the [Arabic alphabet](/arabic-alphabet-guide), several letters will feel familiar in name and order: alef and alif, bet and ba, dalet and dal, lamed and lam, mem and mim, nun and nun. Both scripts descend, through Aramaic, from the same ancient Semitic alphabet.

Two of these sounds have no English equivalent. Het (ח), and kaf without its dot, sound like the *ch* in Scottish *loch*. Resh (ר) is said in the throat, closer to a French *r* than an English one.

## The five final forms (sofit)

Five letters change shape when they are the last letter of a word. The final form is called *sofit*, "final". The sound stays the same, except that final kaf and final pe are always the soft sounds, *kh* and *f*.

| Letter | Final form | Name | Example word | Rank | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| כ | ך | kaf | דרך | 156 | through, via |
| מ | ם | mem | שלום | 137 | peace |
| נ | ן | nun | זמן | 87 | time |
| פ | ף | pe | סוף | 253 | end |
| צ | ץ | tsadi | עץ | 917 | tree |

Four of the five final forms, ך (kaf), ן (nun), ף (pe) and ץ (tsadi), stretch down below the line, where the regular forms sit on it. Final mem (ם) is the odd one out: it stays on the line and closes into a box. When you see a long tail dropping below the line, you are usually at the end of a word.

## Hebrew letters that look alike

Printed Hebrew letters are mostly built from the same square strokes, so some pairs differ by a single corner or a gap. These are the ones to learn side by side.

| Letter | Looks like | Also | How to tell them apart |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ב bet | כ kaf | | Bet's base line sticks out past the corner on the right; kaf is rounded, with no corner. |
| ד dalet | ר resh | | Dalet's top line sticks out past the corner, making a sharp angle; resh is a smooth curve. |
| ה he | ח het | ת tav | He has a gap between its left leg and the roof; het is closed; tav is closed and its left leg ends in a small foot. |
| ו vav | ז zayin | ן final nun | Vav is a short stroke with a small hook; zayin's head sticks out on both sides; final nun is a vav that drops below the line. |
| ס samekh | ם final mem | | Samekh is rounded at the bottom; final mem is square. |

A few letters also **sound** alike in modern Hebrew even though they look different: tet (ט) and tav (ת) are both *t*; qof (ק) and kaf with its dot are both *k*; het (ח) and kaf without its dot are both *kh*; samekh (ס) and shin as *s* are both *s*; and alef (א) and ayin (ע) are both silent for most speakers. Reading them is easy. Spelling them means learning each word.

## The dagesh: the dot that changes the sound

A dagesh is a dot written inside a letter. On most letters it has no effect on modern pronunciation, but on three it changes the sound:

| With the dot | Sound | Without the dot | Sound | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| בּ | b | ב | v | בית (bayit, house) / טוב (tov, good) |
| כּ | k | כ | kh | כן (ken, yes) / ככה (kacha, thus: k first, then kh) |
| פּ | p | פ | f | פה (po, here) / ספר (sefer, book) |

Here is the catch: most Hebrew text leaves the dagesh out along with the vowels, so you often can't see which sound it is. A rule of thumb gets you most of the way. At the start of a word, these letters are usually the hard sound (*b*, *k*, *p*); after a vowel, in the middle or at the end of a word, they are usually soft (*v*, *kh*, *f*). So כלב (dog, #635) is *kelev*: the kaf at the start is *k*, the bet at the end is *v*. There are exceptions, such as אבא (father, #104), said *aba* with a hard *b*, and those you learn word by word.

Shin has a dot of its own: on the upper right (שׁ) it is *sh*, on the upper left (שׂ) it is *s*. Unpointed text leaves that out too.

## Niqqud: the vowel points

Hebrew vowels are written as niqqud, small dots and dashes mostly under the letters. Here they are on mem (מ):

| Sign on מ | Name | Sound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| מַ | patach (a line below) | ma |
| מָ | kamatz (a small T below) | ma, sometimes mo |
| מֶ | segol (three dots below) | me |
| מֵ | tsere (two dots below) | me |
| מִ | hiriq (one dot below) | mi |
| מֹ | holam (a dot above) | mo |
| מֻ | kubutz (three slanted dots below) | mu |
| מוּ | shuruk (a vav with a dot) | mu |
| מְ | shva (two dots, one above the other) | m, or a short e |

### Why most Hebrew text leaves them out

Newspapers, books for adults, street signs, websites and text messages are written without niqqud. Fluent readers recognize the words, and unpointed spelling adds extra vav and yod letters to hint at the vowels. Niqqud appears in children's books, poetry, prayer books and the Bible, dictionaries and textbooks for learners, and occasionally on a single word that would otherwise be ambiguous.

Ambiguity is real. The three letters ספר can be read as *sefer* (book), *safar* (he counted), *siper* (he told) or *sapar* (barber). In a sentence, only one reading makes sense.

<Callout type="info">Learn to read with niqqud first, so you can pronounce what you see, then switch to plain text as your vocabulary grows. Our common words list shows words without niqqud, the way you will meet them in real life, so reading it is good practice once you know the letters.</Callout>

## FAQ

### How many letters are in the Hebrew alphabet?

22. Five of them also have a final form, so there are 27 shapes to learn in print.

### Does Hebrew have vowels?

Yes, but they are written as niqqud, dots and dashes around the letters, and most everyday text leaves them out. The letters vav and yod also stand for some vowels.

### Is Hebrew written right to left?

Yes, words and sentences run right to left. Numbers are written with the largest digit on the left, as in English.

### What is a dagesh in Hebrew?

A dot inside a letter. On bet, kaf and pe it changes the sound: with it they are b, k and p, without it v, kh and f.

### Are the Hebrew and Arabic alphabets related?

Yes. Both are abjads, both run right to left, and they share an ancestor, so several letters have similar names and the same order. The shapes are different, and Arabic letters join while Hebrew letters stand apart. See our [Arabic alphabet guide](/arabic-alphabet-guide).
