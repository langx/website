The best apps to practice Japanese with native speakers are **HelloTalk**, which has a large community in Japan and East Asia, and **Tandem**, for steady one-to-one partners. **LangX** suits learners who want every message open to correction and no ads, **HiNative** is good for quick "does this sound natural?" questions, and **italki** is the place for a paid Japanese tutor. For the reading side, pair them with **Anki** or **WaniKani**.

Japanese asks more of a language exchange than most languages do. You are juggling three scripts, a choice between polite and casual speech in every sentence, and a partner who may be too polite to correct you unless you ask. This guide picks apps with that in mind.

We make LangX, so read our entry with that in mind. Everything about other apps was checked against their own sites and store listings at the time of writing (September 2026). For exchange apps across every language, see [the best language exchange apps](/best-language-exchange-apps).

## How to pick an app to practice Japanese

**Check how the app handles Japanese script.** At the start you cannot read most kanji. Tools that show readings or transliteration, built-in translation, and read-aloud all make a real difference, far more than they do for Spanish or French.

**Think about where Japanese speakers actually are.** An exchange only works if native speakers of Japanese who are learning your language are on the same app. That favors large apps with a strong presence in Asia.

**Decide between writing and speaking.** Japanese learners often understand far more in writing, with time to look things up, than in live speech. Text and voice messages are a sensible first step; live calls with a partner or tutor come next.

**Plan for the long haul.** The US Foreign Service Institute puts Japanese in its hardest category for English speakers, alongside Arabic, Chinese and Korean (see our post on [the easiest and hardest languages](/easiest-languages-to-learn-for-english-speakers)). Pick apps you will still enjoy using in a year.

## Language exchange apps for Japanese

### HelloTalk

[HelloTalk](https://www.hellotalk.com) is based in Shenzhen, China, and its roots in East Asia show: according to its Wikipedia entry, more than half of its users come from China, Japan, South Korea and the United States. For Japanese learners that makes it the most practical starting point.

- **Best for:** finding Japanese partners quickly. Transliteration and translation inside the chat help with kanji you cannot read yet, and **Moments** lets several native speakers correct a short post.
- **Keep in mind:** the free tier has ads and caps on translation and other extras; VIP removes them. The social-feed format is not for everyone.

Compare: [LangX vs HelloTalk](/open-source-alternative-to-hellotalk).

### Tandem

[Tandem](https://tandem.net) reviews new members and is built around one-to-one conversations with text, voice messages and calls. It suits learners who want one or two regular Japanese partners rather than a busy feed.

- **Best for:** a steady weekly exchange partner.
- **Keep in mind:** approval can take a while, and some filters and unlimited translation sit in Tandem Pro.

Compare: [LangX vs Tandem](/open-source-alternative-to-tandem), or see [Tandem vs HelloTalk](/tandem-vs-hellotalk).

### HiNative

[HiNative](https://hinative.com), made by Lang-8, Inc., is not an exchange app but a question-and-answer community: you ask "What's the difference between these two words?" or "Does this sound natural?" and native speakers answer. You can attach audio for pronunciation feedback. It is excellent for the small nuance questions Japanese is full of. Note that at the time of writing its iOS app was last updated in July 2024.

## Tutors and AI for Japanese speaking practice

**[italki](https://www.italki.com)** has many Japanese teachers, from professionals who follow a textbook or prepare you for the JLPT to community tutors who simply keep the conversation going. It no longer offers a free partner search. See [LangX vs italki](/open-source-alternative-to-italki).

## Japanese-specific tools worth adding

**[Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net)** is a free, open-source flashcard app with spaced repetition, and AnkiDroid brings it to Android. Many Japanese learners use it for vocabulary. The best cards come from your own conversations: every correction your partner makes is a card.

**[WaniKani](https://www.wanikani.com)**, from the Tofugu team, teaches kanji through radicals, mnemonics and spaced repetition across 60 levels, and the first levels are free to try. It is a web app focused only on kanji and vocabulary, so it complements an exchange rather than replacing one.

**A good dictionary.** Jisho.org is a widely used free online Japanese-English dictionary. Keep it open while you chat.

## Politeness levels in a Japanese language exchange

This is the part textbooks underplay and exchanges put front and center.

- **Polite form (desu/masu) is the safe default.** Use it with anyone new. You will rarely sound wrong being polite with a stranger.
- **Casual form comes by agreement.** Once you have been chatting for a while, many partners will suggest switching to casual speech, or simply start using it. Follow their lead rather than going casual first.
- **You do not need advanced keigo for an exchange.** The honorific and humble forms matter at work and in customer service. In a chat between peers, polite form is plenty.
- **Corrections may be about register.** If your partner "corrects" a grammatically fine sentence, they may be telling you it is too stiff or too casual for the situation. Ask which one it is.
- **Never add -san to your own name.** It goes after other people's names.

Japanese partners may be reluctant to correct you, since pointing out mistakes can feel impolite. Ask for it clearly and more than once, and thank them when they do.

## Your first message in Japanese

Keep it short and polite. Romanization is in brackets (Hepburn style).

> こんにちは！はじめまして。サムです。日本語を勉強しています。よろしくお願いします。

(Konnichiwa! Hajimemashite. Samu desu. Nihongo o benkyō shite imasu. Yoroshiku onegaishimasu.)

In English: "Hello! Nice to meet you. I'm Sam. I'm studying Japanese. I look forward to talking with you." Yoroshiku onegaishimasu has no neat English equivalent; it is the standard way to close an introduction.

It is fine to add a line in English saying what you can help with and asking them to correct your Japanese. Then a few phrases you will use constantly:

- **ありがとうございます** (arigatō gozaimasu): thank you.
- **もう一度お願いします** (mō ichido onegaishimasu): once more, please.
- **ゆっくりお願いします** (yukkuri onegaishimasu): slowly, please.

For topics beyond introductions, see [language exchange conversation topics](/language-exchange-conversation-topics), and for a clear structure for each session, [how to do a language exchange](/how-to-do-a-language-exchange).

## Tips for Japanese learners on exchange apps

**Learn hiragana and katakana before your first exchange.** Each has 46 basic characters, and a week or two of practice makes chat far easier. Partners are happy to write in kana if you ask, but they will need you to read it.

**Install a Japanese keyboard.** A Japanese input method lets you type in romaji and convert to kana and kanji. Writing back in Japanese script, even simply, gets you much better corrections.

**Treat voice messages as listening practice.** Ask your partner to send a short voice message with the text underneath. Listen first, then read.

**Keep a correction notebook.** Japanese corrections tend to be subtle: a particle, a verb ending, a more natural word. Write them down or turn them into Anki cards the same day.

## FAQ

### What is the best app to practice Japanese with native speakers?

HelloTalk is usually the easiest place to find Japanese partners, because a large share of its community is in East Asia. Tandem is better for a calmer one-to-one exchange, LangX for corrections on every message with no ads, and italki if you want a paid teacher.

### Should I use polite or casual Japanese in a language exchange?

Start with polite desu/masu forms. Switch to casual speech when your partner suggests it or starts using it themselves.

### Can I practice Japanese speaking for free?

Yes. HelloTalk and Tandem include free calls with exchange partners. LangX supports voice messages but not live calls. Tutors on italki are paid.

### Do I need to read Japanese before using an exchange app?

Not strictly, since translation and romanization help at first, but learning hiragana and katakana first makes every conversation easier and earns you better corrections.
